## **THE 19TH CENTURY PHILIPPINES**

As to what era the 19th century refers to requires thorough clarification, in order to establish a common point of reference, especially when it comes to selecting which historical events needed enquiry. One also needs to understand that historians have differing bases and ways of plotting historical timeline. The most common basis is the accurate time period which starts from year 1 to 99\. There are also others who plot only the important events and significant highlights instead of the timeline, to demarcate the centuries. The latter was used by the historians who considered the 19th century as beginning from 1815 during the Congress of Vienna, which ended in 1914 at the beginning of the first world war. The British historian Eric Hobsbawm believed the 19th century started in 1789, the beginning of the French Revolution, and ended in 1914\. But this chapter shall utilize the most common way of demarcating years, thus looking at the 19th century from 1801 to 1900\.

It is necessary to speak about the 19th century because this was when Rizal was born. In order to get to know Rizal better including the things he fought for, it is important to look back on the years he lived through. But another much heavier reason would be the truth that the 19th century paved the way to Filipino nationalism. There were numerous uprisings even before Fr. Burgos continued what Padre Pelaez had started, on the plight of the rights of the natives and common dwellers of the Philippines. Most of the earliest uprisings, however, fought only for the rights of the few, and not of the entire Filipino. For example, the Rajah Sulayman and Lakan Dula 1574 revolt was fronted by the dwellers of Manila and only for the sake of themselves. The 19th century bore witness to the first and only Philippine Revolution, based upon Filipino nationalism. Before the time of Bonifacio, Aguinaldo, and their compatriots, there were numerous rebellions against the colonial government of Spain (Porlomenos) especially against abusive officials, but most of them were only rooted upon personal issues, like that of Francisco Dagohoy in 1744 whose uprising was grounded upon unkept promises; the Tagalog Revolt of Rajah Sulayman and Lakan Dula was based upon religious contempt, like the Tamblot uprising in Bohol, and Bankaw of Leyte in 1621\.

The nationalist sentiment which sparked the 1896 Philippine Revolution, started not from the struggle for independence, but from the anticipation for a much better life of all those who live in the Philippines. Philippine nationalism was not rooted from the hope for liberation, but for the hope that each country dweller shall experience the same benefit of the Filipinos who are in Europe. Looking back into history, more or less 280 years have passed since the Philippines was, ostensibly, discovered by Magellan in 1521, or more than 236 years have passed since Legazpi instituted the first Spanish community in 1565 at Cebu. The history of the Philippines under the Spanish regime spreads vastly; in a span of 236 uninterrupted years, Spain ruled the Philippines, besides the 2 years that the archipelago was colonized by the British wherein both Spaniard Simon de Anda y Salazar and Brit Dawson Drake were holding the administration. It is therefore more understandable why people longed for progress, especially that Spain was one of the most prolific nations, if not the most, during that era. But what happened was quite the contrary.

Ever since Legazpi brought the West into the country, the Philippines was coined Medieval Europe. This was when its neighboring countries were still in a primitive state, especially insofar as technology, education, and commerce were concerned. But when Europe became modernized, the Philippines remained Medieval. If there is one to put the blame on as to why the Philippines never got the progress it has long since aspired for, it was not the friars, or even the governor general. Yes, they could take part in the blame, but the root cause of the non-progress was the feudal system brought by the European conquistadores.

The feudal system was initially for the common good; this system was drafted after the manorial system of the Romans. The common concept of the manorial system is that those who dwell within the manors or large territories \- have rights to rule, in line with the protection they enjoy. At that time, feudalism was centered on the fiefs or feudum, from where the term feudalism was coined. A fief is the share of the land granted to a person called the agalon or seigneur, by the vassal. The vassal gives service to the seigneur in the form of, mostly, military protection especially in times of war and conflict. What usually happens is that the vassal cannot live up to the propriety granted unto him. Because of this, he then confers his services to the tenant vassal. The vassal asks for some share from the tenant vassal, usually through military service or a portion of the property. But the tenant vassal does not thoroughly look after the land. He hires a commoner, usually those who dwell within the property, who will cultivate the land in exchange for food or shelter. These people are called serfs. When the seigneur asks for too much payment from the vassal, the vassal in turn would ask for too much payment from the tenant vassal. In the end, the serfs end up in hunger, because his basic needs would no longer be met, or they end up being the first ones to suffer and die in battle as part of their military service, because they are offered little to no protection compared to the soldiers of the vassal and the tenant vassal. What used to be a fair and just system ended up becoming abusive.

On August 4, 1789, when the French Revolution began, the National Assembly of France eradicated the feudal system. One of the reforms was on fair tax collection. Before the Revolution, the nobles, including the clerics, had tax exemptions. Because of this and because tax collection was highly essential, those at the lowest tier of the society bore most of the financial burden.

Another reform was that of fair retribution. Before the Revolution, punishment, including the gravity of the punishment, was only based upon the preference of the one who gave the punishment. Stated in the Fundamental Principles of Policing and Justice of the French Revolution was that the gravity of the punishment be based upon the law and not on the preference of the powerful.

The French Revolution also totally eradicated seigneurial rights and tithes. Seigneurial rights refer to the fees the nobility was due. Before the Revolution, peasants needed to pay feudal dues out of their lodging in the seigneurial lands. Tithes refer to clerical fees. Before the Revolution, those who belonged to the Third State (or the commoners, blatantly-speaking), needed to pay the clerical tax amounting to ![][image1] of their earnings, to the First State (or the Church).

Because of the astounding events in France, many longed for the elimination of feudalism in their own nations. Spain, however, remained to be Feudal until the Constitucion Politica de la Monarquia Española \- or the Constitution of Cadiz was established on March 19, 1812\. Even after that, there were still remnants of the feudal systems in Spain. These were carried over to the colonies including the Philippines. Even if it already strayed far from its original state, the feudal system prevailed in the 19th century Philippine society.

It is ironic how the system which was supposed to uplift the common man became the primary reason why the country's progress became elusive, even in the presence of wealth and power of the one who turned it into its colony.

## **PYRAMID: THE SOCIAL STRUCTURE**

One of the concrete faces of feudalism can be seen in the social stratification or the classification of peoples based upon dynasty and socio-economic abilities & capacities. The perfect image of the social structure of the 19th century Philippines is a pyramid where the few on the top tier owned most of the properties, whereas the majority on the lowest tier owned whatever else remained, or none at all.

This pyramid was divided into three tiers:

The peninsulares occupy the top level. The term peninsulares refer to the Spaniards born in the Iberian Peninsula; they were made up of government officials and the friars. Lower than the peninsulares are the insulares or creoles. The term insulares refer to the Spaniards born in the Insula or the colonial islands such as the Philippines. Most of the time, there was a heavy rivalry between peninsulares and insulares, mostly because the former did not want the latter to be associated with them. Thus, the peninsulares addressed the insulares not as Spanish but as 'Spanish-Filipinos', which later on became mere 'Filipinos'. Due to such contention, grievances arose from the hearts of the insulares towards the peninsulares. This was probably the reason why many of the insulares, if not all, favored the illustrados and the principalias.

In the middle level lay the mestizos, illustrados, and principales. Mestizos refer to people of different ancestral origins. There are three different types of mestizos: the mestizo de sangley were those of Chinese descent, the mestizo de Espanyol were of Spanish blood, and the tornatras are of mixed Spanish and Chinese origins. Based on this description, Rizal was a tornatra, but commonly, he had Chinese and mestizo de sangley origins. He also had Spanish blood, thereby making him a mestizo de Espanyol, based on his mother's lineage.

The illustrados, literally translated as the enlightened, was the term used for the educated few. Most of them were mestizo de Espanyol, but there were also a number of insulares. They lobbied for change, most especially for fair political and economic governance. Rizal was also considered an illustrado.

The principalias were the noble class before the arrival of the Spaniards. Those who belonged to this faction were the descendants of the datu, rajah, lakan, and others along similar designation. Principales was also the term used to refer to the descendants of the tribal leaders of the balangays or barangays before the arrival of the Spaniards. Some of them even held lower positions, such as gobernadorcillo or cabeza de barangay, in the colonial government. The Rizal clan were also renowned principales.

The low tier was composed of the indigents, also popularly known as indios. The term indio was used to address the natives dwelling in the colonies such as the Philippines. Like what was aforementioned, the term 'Filipino' used to refer solely to the insulares. The indios were the descendants of the Indonesians and Malays who were the first inhabitants of the islands of an archipelago later on coined the Philippines, way before the arrival of the Chinese and the Spaniards. Because Juan Mercado, the grandfather of Rizal, registered their family under the indio category, Rizal was also considered indio.

The Muslims, the mountain tribes from the highlands barely touched by colonialism, and the nomads called the aetas, were considered the marginalized or not part of the society.

The pyramid was noticeably based upon descent, especially when looking into the distinction between the peninsulares and the insulares, and the difference between the top level compared to the middle and the bottom tiers. Come to think of it, most of the recognized mestizo ended up marrying the principales. And because they were of wealthy influence, most of them, or their children, became illustrados.

However, before the arrival of Spain and before this system of society was established, the natives were already stratified into different levels during the time of the datu and lakan. The social structure was nothing new to the principales. The principales, however, used to belong to the top tier, but upon the arrival of the Spaniards, they sank downward the middle class...

Because of the pyramid, social discrimination was evident. Like ancestry, discrimination was evident within the relationship between the peninsulares and the non-peninsulares, also between the middle and low tiers. Austin Craig spoke about the abuses the indios had under the hands of the gobernadorcillios and capitan municipals. Rizal also expressed his dismay and resentment against the mestizo de sangley who were mostly abusive and repulsive against the indios. As such, one of the reasons why the Propaganda Movement failed to push through was the truth that the propagandists, who were illustrados, were fighting only for the interests of the middle class.

After looking into the social structure and the discrimination it unintentionally induced, Rizal's sense of nationalism may have slowly been knitted and come to surface. The question, however, is that, among the mestizo, principalia, illustrado, and indio, for whom did Rizal fight for? Were the Muslims and other indigents included in his plight?

## **THE 19TH CENTURY PHILIPPINE POLITICAL SYSTEM**

If placed side by side along the social structure, feudalism can be concretely seen in the political climate of the 19th century. The political system back then was centralized. If there was one cold truth that can be observed in almost every centralized government, it is the fact that those placed at the top priority are only those closest to power, whilst those who stray far are often forgotten and neglected. This was what happened to the Philippines. Most of the time, Spain had no time to address the needs of the colony, and if ever grievances were heard, the rulers could no longer trace whether the reforms were strictly implemented because of the geographic distance between Spain and the Philippines.

Considering the technical aspect, the Central Government, the National Government, and the Local Government comprise the political system of the 19th Century Philippines. The national government is subjected to the central government, and the local government is subjected to both the national and the central.

These are what make up the Central Government:

First, the Spanish monarch \- the highest civil authority was headed by a king or a queen. He/she holds one of the highest spiritual authorities, like the first patron or sponsor of evangelism. One of them was King Philip II, the Spanish king at the time of the Philippine colonization by Legazpi in 1565\. He was also to whom the Philippines was named after by Villalobos, back when he was still known as Prince Philip of Asturias. Another was Carlos IV who ruled during the 19th century. Isabela was the queen who declared the 1863 Educational Decree. Alfonso XIII was the ruler during the beginning of the Philippine fight for independence from Spain.

Next, The Royal and Supreme Council of the Indies. They were the appointed territorial administrators outside of Spain, known as the Indies. The usual heads of this council were priests or clerics. The council was established in 1524 by King Carlos V, which lasted only until 1834 after Maria Christina, regent and mother of Queen Elizabeth II, abolished it. Six to ten royal councilors, appointed by the king or queen, comprised the council. It served as the Court of Appeals to the colonies. Their job was to turn into official laws the decisions and legislations regarding the territories of Spain, which was collectively called the Laws of the Indies.

Third, the Viceroy of Nueva España. He was the ruler of the kingdom of Nueva España, including the Spanish territories in North America, South America, Asia, and Oceania, as a representative of the King. The Viceroyalty of Nueva España was established on August 18, 1521, and was based in Mexico. The Philippines was looked after by the viceroy from 1565 until Mexico and other territories of Central America declared independence in 1821\. Francisco Ceinos was the viceroy at the time of the colonization in 1565, \[missing text\]

Fourth, the Ministerio de Ultramar. The Ministry for Overseas Territories was the department that gave mandates, on behalf of the king, towards the remaining territories of Spain after Mexico and other territories gained independence. Before this department was established, the administration of the territories were under the Ministry of the Navy. The Minesterio de Ultramar was abolished and its power was taken away on April 20, 1899\. For the Filipinos, the most important minister of this department was Don Antonio Maura, who established the Maura Law, which became the path to the reorganization of the local government; making it more autonomous and effective.

Next to the Central Government was the National Government. This was the direct administrator of the colonies. The government officials that comprise this are:

1. Captain General/Governor-General. The Captaincy General is a constituent of the Viceroyalty that oversees threats to colonization from other nations, or if the established colonies have not yet been pacified. The Captaincy General of the Philippines was under the governance of the Viceroyalty of Mexico, and this was what served as Spain's center of administration called Spanish East Indies. The Spanish East Indies were the territories covering the Philippine islands, including the islands of the Carolinas which include what are now called Palau, Gilbert Islands, and Marshall Islands. Appointed by the King through the Viceroy and as advised by the Royal and Supreme Council of the Indies, the Captain General held the executive power as a governador, including legislative power because he had the authority to promulgate executive decrees and ordinances. He also held judicial powers because he was the head or the President of the Real Audiencia, and even ecclesiastical power because he was the Vice Patron of the colony. Some noteworthy Governor-Generals include El Adelantado Miguel Lopez de Legazpi who led the expedition that conquered the Philippine Islands; Rafael Maria de Aguilar y Ponce de Leon who ruled the beginning of the 19th century Philippines; Carlos Maria de la Torre who was known for being liberal and who brought the idea of liberalism to the Philippines; Narciso Claveria y Zaldua who issued a mandate that citizens of the Philippines be given surnames; Rafael de Izquierdo who ruled at the time of the Cavite Mutiny; Fernando Primo de Rivera who temporarily halted the revolution by establishing the Pact of Biak-na-Bato with Aguinaldo who favored reform and Emilio Terrero who was loathed by the friars and who became instrumental to the non-banning of Rizal's novels; Eulogio Despujol who was also a liberal and under whose term Rizal was exiled to Dapitan, Ramon Blanco who expressed the intention to have a dialogue with the revolutionaries for the sake of peace but ironically put the provinces of Manila, Bulacan, Cavite, Pampanga, Tarlac, Laguna, Batangas, and Nueva Ecija under Martial Law; Camilo Garcia de Polavieja who arranged and oversaw the court martial regarding the execution of Rizal; and Basilio Augustin under whose term Philippines was sold by Spain to the United States.
2. The Archbishop/Bishop of Manila. He ruled the Roman Catholic Diocese of Manila after its establishment as a suffragan of the Archdiocese of Mexico in 1579, until it became the Archdiocese of Manila in 1595\. As a ruler of the local church, the bishop or archbishop holds the highest authority in his diocese. Come to think of it, he responded to no other authority except for the Pope. But during the time of conquest, the bishops were also subjected to the King. Most of them were actually appointed to their different dioceses, by the King, like the first Bishop of Manila, Dominican friar Fray Domingo de Salazar. Because of this, the Bishop held great political influence beyond his spiritual authority. He had the power as the Vice General of the colony, and he had the full spiritual authority over the army and the navy. There were times when the Bishop/Archbishop showed much greater political influence compared to the Governor-General. Such was the case with the Dominican Archbishop of Manila, Bernardino Nozaleda y Villa. Along with other influential Dominicans, they overthrew Governor-General Ramon Blanco. Consequently, four Bishops/Archbishops became acting Governor- General. They were Archbishop Francisco de la Cuesta who became Acting Governor General after the execution of Governor General Fernando Manuel de Bustillo Bustamante in 1745; Bishop Miguel Lino de Ezpeleta after the demise of Pedro Manuel de Arandia Santiseban in 1759; and Archbishop Manuel Rojo del Rio who took over de Ezpleta after he was removed from office by King Carlos III.
3. The Cabo Segundo. This position was inaugurated in 1762 after the controversy surrounding Archbishop Manuel de Rojo del Rio who surrendered Manila to the British Invaders, and Simon de Anda y Salazar who was later granted the title Lieutenant Governor of Manila. To refrain from controversy, the Cabo Segundo automatically took over the Governor General position in the case of death.
4. The Intendant General. The office of the Intendant General was held by the Bourbon of Spain back when the Bourbon Dynasty ruled over the land. Drafted after France, this office emerged from the so-called Bourbon Reforms. Directly appointed by the King or Queen, the primary obligation of the Intendant General was to look after the whereabouts of the officials.
5. The Real Audiencia de Manila. Established in 1583, the Audiencia served as the Supreme Court of the colony. This also served as the adviser of the Captain General. At first, the Real Audiencia was composed of 4 Huwes kun Oidores, 1 Attorney-General or Fiscal, and 1 Constable. They were also the so-called defenders of the accused, protectors of the naturales or indigentia, and other officials. Overtime, the Oidores and the fiscal system increased in number. There was also a time when the Audiencia held the government in the case of the death of the governor or mayor, until they could find a replacement. The Governor-General was considered the highest judge. There were also officials from the Real Audiencia who became Governor General. First among them was Cristobal Tellez de Almanza. Simon de Anda was also an Oidor.

Below the national government was the local government. The officials that comprise this were:

1. Provincia/Alcaldia. Ruled by the Provincia Mayor or Alcalde Mayor, this was the political territory in the absence of conflict. He held the executive and judiciary powers within his province. The Provincia or Alcalde Mayor was also appointed as tax collector. They were also given the "indulto para komersyo" until the middle of the 19th century.
2. The Suffragan Dioceses. These were the dioceses established to assist the Archdiocese such as the Dioceses of Nueva Caceres, Nueva Segovia, and Cebu to accompany the Archdiocese of Manila. The bishops who ruled these also held political influence.
3. The Corregimiento. These were the provincial districts before the colonization. and where there were still threats of conflict. These were ruled by the Corregidor who worked under the alcalde mayor as the administrator and judge of the districts in question.
4. The Junta Provincial. This was the provincial council whose task was to help the alcalde mayor as a public prosecutor, and financial adviser, among others.
5. The Gobernadorcillo. This was the administrator of the cities. At first, those who can become a gobernadorcillo were only the married principalia men, but since 1768, the position can already be filled through election.
6. The Captain Municipal. This became the substitute for the title gobernadorcillo based upon the Maura Law. A wider authority was vested upon the Captain Municipal compared to the gobernadorcillo, because the former can already build infrastructure without asking for approval from Manila. According to the famous German ethnologist Feodor Jagor, based on the election he witnessed, the one who can garner the majority of the votes from 13 voters consisted of 6 cabeza de barangay, 6 former gobernadorcillo and current gobernadorcillo, becomes the Captain Municipal. Part of its task was being the helping hand of the Provincia Mayor and the Kura Paroko. Advising and suspending officials, collecting taxes, and serving as the judge of his territory were only a few of the obligations of the Captain Municipal. They also served as the ruler of the municipal tribunal.
7. The Tribunal Municipal. This municipal council was composed of the captain municipal and his assistants who were the Lieutenant Mayor, Teniente de Policia, Teniente de Sementeras, and the Teniente de Ganados.
8. The Alcaldes Ordinarios. Termed as Alcalde de Primer Voto (Mayor) and Alcalde de Segundo Voto (Vice-Mayor), they served as the judge and administrators of the Ayuntamiento or Cabildo (City Council).
9. The Cabeza de Barangay. These were the administrators of the barangay, composed of 40-50 families. The job of the cabeza is to collect tribute. At first, this position was held by the members of the principalia. But in 1786, this became elective. The gobernadorcillo and some of the cabeza get to select one person to be appointed by the Governor General. After a 3-year term, the cabeza becomes qualified for the position of Captain Municipal.

These were the political officials that comprised the 19th century Philippines. All the necessary elements and mechanisms were present, to ensure good governance. There were executive, legislative, juridical, and advisory councils. They imitated the ancient Romans' way of governing vast empires such as the establishment of viceroyalties and captaincies general. There were a lot of officials appointed to secure peace and order even under the lowest ranks. But how come there still was, ironically absence of peace and order?

## **CENTRALIZED MACHINE: WEAKNESSES AND ABUSES IN THE POLITICS AND ECONOMY**

As proof that the governance was contrary to the aspired peace and order, the administrative problem of the Spanish colonial government will now be presented. The centralized political system encountered a lot of difficulties, and one of which is the administration that was weak and prone to abuse. The sole reason why the administration was weak was because of its officials, from the members of the Royal and Supreme Council of the Indies, up to the Viceroy and the Governor General and even down to the cabeza de barangay. Those who were of the lower ranks, like the gobernadorcillo and cabeza de barangay, were appointed and not elected, not by the citizens but only by the few, approved by the Governor General. In other words, the gobernadorcillo and the cabeza de barangay were appointed by the Governor General. Hence, all officials back then were appointed and not elected. And even if one may say there was nothing wrong with that because most of the higher officials were members of the Spanish nobility, and the noblemen do not automatically entail ability; the fact is that, the officials were appointed not based on some qualification but only mostly because they were favored or because they were of close relations to the officials. Besides the time when the liberal generals ruled the monarchy for a short while, this system resulted in inefficient governance. What's worse, such inefficiency later paved way to grave abuse from the officials.

Another reason for maladministration was the frequent conflict between the officials. The higher officials, because they were appointed by the king, had direct communication with the royalty, straying from the protocol, just like the Governor General who was supposed to receive command from the viceroy. But because the Philippines was geographically far from Mexico and because the Governor General had direct communication with the King, the Governor General no longer follows the command from the Viceroy. In such a way, the Viceroy oftentimes communicated directly with the King and no longer consulted the Supreme Council. This was also what happened with the officials of the lower rank.

Besides this, the truth is, those from the Church, the friars and the seculars were much more powerful and influential compared to the government officials. There were instances when these ecclesial people exercised their power and influence profusely. This was the case with Governor General Ramon Blanco. Blanco showed sympathy towards the indigents and natives, but he was replaced by Camilo Garcia de Polavieja allegedly because of the friars' campaign.

Those were only a few of the many abuses committed. Probably the greatest was the encomienda system. Under this system, the monarchy or the leaders of the conquistadors gave the right to claim tribute (tax) and labor or manpower from the acquired territory to particular individuals or groups. Back in the time of conquest, the colonizers were used to owning all territory under the name of the king. This was the reason why in their perspective they had the right to the producers and the people within their territorial bounds. This Tribute system or taxation brought suffering especially to the indigents whose economic yield was only enough to sustain their daily needs. Another was the issue of forced labor. There was no slavery under the colonial government, but even in the absence of slavery, there was polo y servicio under the encomienda system. With polo y servicio, all males aged 16-60 who had the capacity for labor were obliged to render a 40-day service to the government with their projects such as infrastructures. They can be spared only if they pay falla worth ![][image2] reals \- a quantity nearly impossible to be afforded by the ordinary. Even when the tribute was abolished and later on superseded by other taxes, abuse still persisted. The local officials of the alcaldia who were responsible for tax collection usually took more than what was required, as the excess went to their own pockets. There were dues that needed to be paid to the national government. And even with the privilege to own businesses, the indulto para commerciar was also abused. The officials sold the indulto for higher prices. Because the alcalde mayor or provincial mayor was both administrator and judge, there was no way to end the corruption. As the judge, he processed the complaints regarding him as an administrator. This was always the case back when the office of the civil government was not yet established. The office of the civil government served as the administrator, and the remaining power of the alcalde mayor was juridical. At this point, it is necessary to bear in mind that the justice system at that time was in itself abusive. The favored ones were of course the rich and powerful, over the commoners. The poor who cannot even pay for the litigation were automatically declared guilty even in the absence of trial and due process. But even the wealthy and powerful also experienced abuse especially if they were in conflict with the wealthier and more powerful. This was the case with Rizal's mother Daña Teodora who was framed up by her brother's wife along with rwy local offi \[unclear\]

There were existing offices commissioned to foresee the corrupt ways of the Governor General and other officials such as the Residencia whose task was to evaluate the Governor General by the end of his term, the Visita where the monarchy sent the so-called Visitador General to visit the Governor General in the colonies unannounced, and the Intendencia where the so-called Intendant General foresees all other lower offices. However, these offices overlooked the abuses because they were as corrupt, if not more, as the officials they were supposed to assess.

## **OBSOLETE AND REPRESSIVE? THE EDUCATIONAL SYSTEM**

Besides the political and the economical, the internal problem with the political setup of the colonial government can be seen in the education system. This was because (1) the people of the church held high power in the government, and (2) they oftentimes were more powerful than the civil officials.

Since the beginning of the Spanish colonization, two institutions were prevalent: the church and the schools. Based upon the mandate of the king, particularly King Felipe II, along with the mandate of the church regarding educating the indigents in the name of Christianity, the friars together with the conquistadors established schools aside from churches. Which was why in 1565, at the advent of Spanish colonization to the Philippines, Augustinian friars built the first school at Cebu. Because education was solely for the purpose of converting the natives into Catholics, the first teachers were the friars. In a sense, education became an instrument for the indigents to obey the church. Some would even say that schools were established by the friars as a plot to control the natives.

But this, based upon the research of the Jesuit historian Henry Frederick Fox, must not be a subject of dismay or become a basis of hatred towards the friars. The Philippines was even deemed fortunate because some colonies from Europe and America were not given the same education.

In 1863, through the Education Reform Decree, the public school system was founded. Under this system, children ages 7 to 13 were given free elementary education and were obliged to attend school. With this, different schools for boys and girls were built. The School for Teachers was also established, and one of which was Escuela Normal de Maestros which later on became the Philippine Normal University. The secondary and tertiary institutions taught, besides religion, secular subjects such as mathematics, physics, and history.

Still, there was discrimination. Free education was only until elementary. Meaning, those who could make it to secondary and tertiary education were only those who could afford it. Even if education was open for all regardless of social status, it was evident how the males were more favored than the females. The men were given priority in tertiary education, and the women were only given vocational courses. The indigents usually experienced discrimination from their classmates and even from their teachers. It must be clear, however, that the discrimination came from their Spanish classmates and teachers, and not from the system itself.

Most importantly, the schools were under the hands of the friars. Even with the challenge of the liberal Governor General Carlos Maria de la Torre and the Overseas Minister Segismundo Moret through the Moret Decree to make the Philippine education system secular, it did not push through because of the protests regarding the steps taken by the Dominican procurator of Madrid, Fray Francisco Rivas, who used his influence as a representative of the Spanish Cortes. This blatantly showed how the power of the friars was far greater than the power of any civil official of the government.

The colonial government of Spain in the Philippines was so prone and open to the possibility of maladministration and abuse. In politics, officials were mostly appointed based on personal favor over ability. Moreover, the officials did not abide by protocols. The geographic distance from the Philippines to Spain was a huge factor because, based on the feudal system, the tenants in the Philippines \- the officials \- ended up running the government instead. The friars were most times more powerful than the civil government and were so adept at exercising their authority. These things led to abuse to the economy, which already carried the burdens of the tribute system and the polo y servicio, and even to the lack of education.

Padre Pelaez, Gomez, and Burgos wanted to address such abuses and deficiencies, including Rizal and other illustrados. But which among those reasons came to be the greatest factor to what has become of the Philippines? Can those factors still be seen in the face of our current politics, economy, and education?

The abuses and shortcomings mentioned were reasons enough why Philippine ancestors longed for transformation. Such hope was aggravated when they witnessed how there might be a promise of a better life beyond the Spanish colonial government.

## **RIPPLES: WORLD EVENTS AND THEIR EFFECTS IN THE COLONIAL SOCIETY**

Whas transpired \[sic\] to world history in the 19th century, which has something to do with the Philippines directly and indirectly, were as follows:

1. Bedemacation. \[unclear\] In 1807, within what was considered the First Industrial Revolution. England built the biggest factory which utilized a steam engine fueled by carbon. Faster and more efficient means of production paved the way to the expansion of industrialization. The year 1870 marked the Second Industrial Revolution. Many started to use the steam engine, including mechanical inventions that resulted in mass production which in turn resulted in unprecedented progress. During the 19th century, the world witnessed a broader use for steam engines. A lot of steamships sailed, both for transportation and for commerce. Human relations became globalized as nations participated in international trade. When the Suez Canal opened in 1869, transactions and relations were made faster and broader. In the same century, because of the progress & development and human relations that centered in the cities, the usage of trains and telegraphs became widespread. The cities also experienced a more modern sewerage system that made waste disposal pass through underground canals. These were but a few of the many revolutionary changes and improvement of the human condition during those times.
2. Political upheaval. The 19th century bore witness to the more progressive aspect of politics. The world witnessed Napoleon Bonaparte granting his constituents the aspirations of the French Revolution such as liberty and brotherhood of men, not only in France but also in its neighboring nations. The same century witnessed the defeat and fall of Bonaparte, which paved the way to the return of monarchy. One of the renowned Irish Rebellions or the fight for liberty of the inhabitants of Ireland against the United Kingdom took place in 1803\. In 1804, the Serbs were granted autonomy from the Ottoman empire. In the same year, Haiti declared independence from France. In 1808, the slow loss of control of Spain over its American colonies began in Venezuela, Bolivia, and Colombia, which finally gained independence in 1811\. England in 1819 called for universal suffrage, which granted everybody the right to vote regardless of social status and gender. In 1820, the Spanish liberals had an uprising. In 1831, revolts in Italy ensued, against the papal states or the territories owned by the church. In 1848, Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels published The Communist Manifesto. All these, except for Karl Marx whose proposition was on class struggles, happened out of the ideology of nationalism. There was also the call for Europe and America to put an end to slavery.
3. The gradual loss of influence of the Catholic Church. In 325, under the Council of Nicaea, Emperor Constantine declared Christianity as the official religion of the Roman Empire. Since then, the Catholic Church held great influence over politics. Even at the collapse of the Western Roman Empire in 476, the influence of the church endured the Eastern Roman Empire until its downfall under the hands of the Ottoman in 1453\. From 325 until 1453, the influence of the church over politics was massive. One of the reasons was the territorial donation made by Pepin the Short to the papacy, so-called Papal States. This was due to the favor granted by Pope Zachary and Pope Stephen II. In 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne, son of Pepin the Short, the Emperor of Rome. As years passed, because of the support of the church towards the divine rights of kings which became the basis of monarchy, the bishops leaned towards the kings, and in turn, the kings appointed people to become bishops. The alliance between the papacy and the kingdom was evident. The priests or bishops stood as high-ranking officials in the kingdom. There were instances when the papacy claimed and showed that it was greater than the emperors and kings, as with what transpired during the Crusades where the kings were obliged to send soldiers out of the request of the Pope. There were a lot of questions regarding the papal supremacy, such as King Henry VIII in 1534 and the Gallicans of France who in 1682 formally declared that the Pope was no greater than the king or the council insofar as politics was concerned. Despite this, the church remained influential over European kingdoms. Then the French Revolution ignited in 1879, which eradicated both the spiritual power of the church and the temporal or secular powers of the king. When Bonaparte ruled France, he merged the Papal States with the French Empire. Even when the Papal States were temporarily granted back to the Church, Bonaparte foresaw that the liberty and equity aimed by the French Revolution and fought for by him were short-lived. The Papal States were totally abolished in 1870 because of the infamous Unification of Italy. Along with the Papal States came the eradication of the temporal or secular power of the church when it comes to politics.

Such world events the progress of industrialization which became the catalyst to the spread of mass production and commerce, the expansion of liberalism and nationalism which became the impetus for the collapse of the kingdom and the empire, and the modification of the church position regarding politics – paved way to the progress and transformation of the Philippine society. The Philippines played an important role in the Age of Mercantilism through the Galleon Trade, whose route connected Manila and Acapulco, Mexico. Commerce expanded, and gradually traders like England entered the archipelago. Because of this, Filipinos developed a sense of comparison between Spain and England, and they saw that the latter was much better. It was not only commercial products that were traded; along came ideas. The liberal ideology slowly penetrated the conservative system of education. Even if the Church remained powerful over both the Philippines and Spain, its influence gradually diminished because of the liberals. The office of Governor General de la Torre, sent by the liberal General of Spain Francisco Serrano, proved to the Filipinos that there were still a few virtuous Spanish officials. Because transport to Europe became more accessible through the steamships and the opening of the Suez Canal, the illustrados went to Europe. There they saw the disparity between the life situation and education in Europe compared to the Philippines. They also encountered in person the liberal officials of the government. Because of this, their desire for equal treatment for those living in Spain and those inhabiting the Philippines got stronger, a plea which they addressed to and wished for both the Spanish government and their fellow countrymen.
