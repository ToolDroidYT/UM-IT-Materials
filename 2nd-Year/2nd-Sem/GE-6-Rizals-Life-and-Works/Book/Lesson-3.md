# **OF PEOPLES AND A PEOPLE: FUNDAMENTAL QUESTIONS AND CONCEPTS ABOUT NATIONALISM**

To recall the Rizal Law's rationale, it is vital to learn about the life and works of Rizal to ignite and fuel his legacy of a sense of nationalism and patriotism to the Filipino people. But what is nationalism? And what is Filipino nationalism? It is important to know the answers to these so one could understand much deeper the nationalism fought for by Rizal. One has to know and understand the gift so that one can accept it in the spirit of its giving.

What will be discussed in particular are the definition of nationalism and the concept of a nation, the peoples called the Filipino based upon the history of the Philippines, and the common Filipino traits and values. In turn, indigenous displacement shall also be dealt with.

The definition of nationalism and the concept of a nation make one understand two distinct but related facets of nationalism: how nationalism is not only ethno-cultural, but also socio-political. Through this, one could grasp the Filipino nationalism of Rizal and his contemporaries even though one still has to hear about Rizal or even Bonifacio's native lineage.

This way, tracing history to acknowledge the people we may consider as Filipinos gives us added understanding why the nationalism of Rizal and his companions was Filipino nationalism even if according to history the term Filipino differs from our current day usage.

A discussion on the Filipino traits will render the closest illustration of the so-called Filipino national identity that until today remains elusive.

The last part on indigenous displacement shall give a glimpse of how the indigent was displaced and estranged, both in physical and cultural aspects.

## **Nationalism: Socio-Political and Ethno-Cultural Aspects**

Historical figures such as the national heroes operate as essential constituents that both support and are supported by narratives of nation. Nationalism, however, is a "closed circuit that demands the paradoxical assertion that despite any acknowledgment of influential outside forces, such as the thrust of global politics or the decay of imperial or colonial control, the nation is nevertheless deemed an autonomous entity with a distinct culture and history" (Valenzuela, 2014). Hence, the people capped as national heroes only post-colonially, as with the case of Rizal and compatriots, are mere constructed figures that may or may not be contradictory or in conflict with the narratives of a nation. Fallaw and Brunk, as quoted by Valenzuela (2014), stated that heroes aid people in identifying with and embodying a nation \- including its principles and laws, which in turn leads to yielding a stronger sense of unity. National heroes, therefore, are a way of simplifying the complexities of nationalism.

But according to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the term nationalism portrays two distinct yet related phenomena:

First, nationalism illustrates the values of each member of a nation, thereby exhibiting expression of national identity. One of the common ways of such expression is how a person finds pride towards where he comes from, including the culture and values he acquired. A concrete example of this is the pride observed from the Japanese who until now still abides by the tradition of leaving shoes outside the house or outside restaurants even if it is unreasonable in the eyes of the foreigners. One can also observe an expression of the Chinese national identity in their annual celebration of festivals like the Lunar New Year which is not only a matter of wishing for good fortune but also a way of paying homage to their ancestors. Other sets of good examples are the pride of using one's mother tongue, such as the Japanese or even the Ilonggos and Mandayas who proudly use their language even in places where they can be considered a minority, the wearing of traditional clothing like the Kimono for the Japanese and Bahag for some of the Filipino natives, while others also express nationalism when it comes to one's feeling of pride for another's success, such as the victories of Pacquiao in the international boxing arena. But the much deeper expression of national identity is found in unity and community. The sense of pride, one's celebration for another's success, to be burdened by the another's worries, individual victory becoming everyone else's, and failure, everybody's downfall, when hand in hand everyone faces the challenges of life for the sake of the same tongue spoken or same place where they all came from.

The second phenomenon refers to the movement of the members of one nation towards their search for and sustenance of liberty or independence. This is true if the interest of one group differs and is stepped upon by a more powerful group. One concrete example was the plea of Rizal to recognize the Philippines as a province and not only as a mere colony of Spain, and to give the Filipinos the same freedom that the Spaniards enjoy. Another was the yearning of Bonifacio to achieve genuine independence as a nation, to recognize the country neither as a province nor a colony, but as a nation of its own. The continuous struggle of the Scottish few to recognize that they are not a different nation but a state independent from the United Kingdom is another example of this second phenomenon, in these current times. Such was the same case with the recent attempt of Catalonia to separate from the kingdom of Spain. At this point, it does not matter if one uses the same language, or if one wears the same attire, or if one shares the same culture with that of others. What is important is being inhabitants of the same nation, and if everyone is sharing the same rights and welfare, and altogether safeguarding that these never be neglected or taken for granted.

Nationalism is not only ethno-cultural, contrary to common knowledge. Nationalism also has socio-political aspect. These two are what comprise the concept of a nation.

The continuing debate as for what makes a nation or "nationhood" is centered upon the ethno-cultural and socio-political. This dispute can be seen in the anthropological definition of Benedict Anderson regarding the term nation as "an imagined political community". And, as mentioned earlier, this is what is commonly referred to by the term nationalism. But based upon its etymology, the term "nation" is from the Middle English _nacioun_ which means a group of people that possesses the same ancestry and language. From the Latin word _natus_ or _natio_, this term refers to genealogy, lineage, and descent. This is where identity and belongingness are anchored from. Some of the ethno-cultural nations are Abazinia in Russia whose inhabitants are called Abazins, Apacheria of the US whose inhabitants are Apache, Arabia in Yemen, Oman, Saudi Arabia, and others, and the nation of the Catalan in Spain and France, Finland whose inhabitants are the Finns, and a lot more. In the Philippines come the nations of the Ivatan, Kapampangan, Visayans, Zamboangueño, Tagalog, and others. It is noticeable how there are nations that are considered countries, and there are nations that comprise several countries, and there are nations that are within a country.

The last category is the clear depiction of the latter aspect of nation \- being socio-political. As a matter of fact, the sole reason why nationalism became an issue was the rise of nationalism movements. Such movements started with groups that hoped to experience independence or those who longed for equal rights within a country. The philosophers Thomas Hobbes, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, and John Locke had discourses on the meaning of sovereignty, that a nation without territorial sovereignty shall sooner or later cease to exist, just like the Rohingya who until now do not have their own territory. This socio-political aspect of the nation can also be clearly seen in groups that have members coming from varied ethno-cultural factions like the Catalan Independence Movement supported not only by the Catalans but also others who dwell in Catalonia. This was what happened to the Philippines at the time of Burgos, Rizal, and Bonifacio. What unified them was not common lineage and language, but the common struggle. Nationalism, then, begins with the ethno-cultural aspect, but it also has the socio-political aspect. If one cannot observe unity in language, one can perceive unity in the face of collective struggle.

## **WHO WAS THE FILIPINO? CHRONICLING THE INHABITANTS OF THE ISLANDS**

The question of who the Filipino is has already been asked by a number. This discourse is but an attempt to establish the Filipino national identity. The particular method to use is to trace back history, for the picture of the Filipinos would not be complete without looking back into the contemporary dwellers of the Philippines.

Back in the Pre-colonial Period or the era when the Europeans had not yet arrived in the archipelago, theories arose on the early settlers: Migration Theory of Henry Otley Beyer, and other recent theories like that of Felipe Landa Jocano's Theory of Evolution.

According to Beyer, the first inhabitants of the Philippine islands were cavemen. They lived solely on hunting and fishing. These people have the resemblance of the _Homo erectus_ whose bones were unearthed in Java and Peking. In this theory, these people called the Dawn Men were from the island that is now part of Indonesia, and arrived at the Philippine island through some land bridge. After them came the group of dark-skinned people called the Aeta or Negrito, at around 30,000 years after, through the land bridge from the lands now part of Malaya, Borneo, and Australia. Like the Dawn Men, they lived on hunting and fishing. They were expert food gatherers. They used stone and spears in hunting and fishing. The Negritos never left because at that time, the ice melted and destroyed the land bridges. Because of this, they were forced to settle in the islands of Palawan, Mindoro, and Mindanao. Next to the Negritos, the group of sea-navigators arrived from the islands of Indonesia at around 5000-6000 after. They were called Indones, who were more technologically advanced compared to the Negritos. Besides stone, they also used steel. They were not dependent on fishing and hunting because they brought with them technology for farming and mining. They also utilized brass, wore clothing and adornment. In 3000 BCE, other groups of fishers arrived from mainland Asia and Indonesia. They were called Malays, whose culture was more advanced. They had technology for melting iron and made objects out of iron. As for agriculture, they also brought technology for rice fields. They came in three sets, each with different ethnicities. The first faction was farmers of the highlands of Luzon such as Benguet and Ifugao. The second set were the ancestors Bikolano, Bisaya, and Tagalog. The third set came from the Muslim-Malays. The culture of the Malay became the dominant culture throughout the islands. They made their society civilized by establishing social and political systems like the _barangay_ system wherein one community comprises members of the same clan, headed by the _datu_. The social structure was divided into 3: the _maginoo_, the _maharlika_ and _timawa_, and the _alipin_. These are what the Spaniards encountered upon their arrival in the ![][image1] century.

Felipe Landa Jocano, however, did not agree with Beyer's theory. According to Jocano, no migration transpired. In his Core Population Theory, Jocano construed that people dwelling in what is now Southeast Asia as part of mainland Asia were sharing a singular culture. The first inhabitants of the islands, then, were not migrants, but original dwellers. The cultural differences were only a result of their going separate ways.

The Age of Exploration transpired in the 15th century. This was the time when the nations of Europe, particularly Spain and Portugal, searched for territories beyond the continent. In the 16th century, the Spaniards arrived in the Philippines. But there was no "Philippines" yet around that time. There were only the chiefdoms, kingdoms, and sultanates headed by the _datu_, _rajah_, _lakan_ or _sultan_, within autonomous territories owned by the surrounding empires and kingdoms. The name _Felipinas_ was first used in 1543 as the term given by the Spaniard Ruy Lopez de Villalobos. _Felipinas_ was what he baptized the islands of Samar and Leyte (but Baganga or Sarangani according to other sources), named after Prince Philip of Asturias. The name _Felipinas_ replaced _Las islas de San Lazaro_ or St. Lazarus' Islands, and _Las Islas de Poniente_ or Islands to the West, the names given by Magellan to the islands of Homonhon in Eastern Samar, and Homonhon Islands, Leyte, and Cebu, in exact order. As was customary, the people who dwelt in those islands were the first Filipinos.

In 1565, Legazpi formally began the Spanish colonization of the Philippines. There were varied accounts regarding who the Filipino was at the time the Philippines was a colony of Spain. First, according to the account of the Jesuit historian Padre Pedro Chirino in 1604 \- _Relacion de las Islas Filipinas_ \- the term Filipino was used to acknowledge the indigents or the natives who dwelled in the Philippines, especially those who abided by the tradition, culture, and values such as faith in the _anitos_ and _diwatas_, or the giving of dowry in marriage. There were also those who say that, at the time the Philippines was under the Spanish colony, the term Filipino was used for the Spaniards born in the Philippines. As with the social structure in the 19th century, the term _españoles filipinos_ was given by the _peninsulares_ to the _insulares_. The first usage of the term Filipino to acknowledge the indigents was recorded, according to Ambeth Ocampo, in Rizal's _A la juventud Filipina_, where the writer used the terms "Fatherland", "Indian Land", and "Filipinas" in speaking about the same thing. The secular priest Jose Burgos also referred to the Philippine indigents as Filipinos. For Apolinario Mabini, the Filipinos were the inhabitants of the Philippines. But who was the Filipino?

## **FILIPINO NATIONAL IDENTITY: COMMON VALUES AND TRAITS**

Such a question is easy if we base the answer on citizenship, stated deliberately in the 1987 Constitution. But insofar as nationalism is concerned, roughly after 70,000 years of evolution, unity and separation, such a question remains to be a challenge. As of now, according to UNDP in 2013, Philippines already has 110 ethno-linguistic nations each of those more different than similar to the others.

But regardless of differences, there are also similarities or commonalities in nature and attitude towards what we find meaningful. Some of these were put into discourse by the famous Filipino philosophers Emerita Quito and Padre Jaime Bulatao, such as _hiya_, _ningas-cogon_, _pakikisama_, _patigasan_, _bahala na_, _kasi_, _sakop_, _mañana_, _utang na loob_, and _kanya-kanya_.

First, the _hiya_. The Tagalog term _hiya_ has different connotations, such as shame and modesty. Shame can be very much observed in people who are so conscious of their actions all because they fear what others might say. On the other hand, modesty carries the essence of fully knowing oneself.

Next, the _ningas-cogon_, which literally illustrates the burning of cogon. As easy as it catches fire, its flame also gets easily smothered. Such depicts our drive towards accomplishment. What is great about _ningas-cogon_, is the striking of iron while it is hot. The problem, however, is sustaining such determination, especially towards long-term goals such as education or contribution to the prospering of society.

As for _pakikisama_, this Tagalog term also has different connotations, such as group loyalty and getting along, or going with the flow. This portrays interpersonal relationships. There are people who do things against their will, as long as it is for a greater cause and it benefits the community. On a personal level, many might have learned to do things just because of peer pressure, like doing vices or choosing a college course. Even in politics, we end up fighting and even dying, for certain political parties. What is unfortunate is, people end up covering up the errors of one, for the sake of maintaining good relations and reputation. As with the first two Filipino traits, _pakikisama_ carries both pros and cons. It is good in a sense that we all need to establish good human relations, especially in achieving common goals. According to the bible, a house that is divided against itself cannot stand. It is fitting for the members of a group to sincerely support the group's causes. The problem, however, is if the group does something wrong, the members end up enabling it. With the previous chapters, we learned that the reason why the colonial administration was abusive was because it was based more on loyalty instead of ability. It is important to determine when and when not to exercise _pakikisama_.

Another common Filipino trait is the _patigasan_ or test of strength. In Binisaya, _pagahiay_. This shows the Filipino tendency to choose pride and self-righteousness over goodwill and living harmoniously. There are friendships that were ruined because of waiting for someone to apologize first. At home, sometimes parents and children argue and the child ends up receiving blows even when he/she is right. Relationships get on the rocks because both parties think they are right. It is not wrong to stand up for what one thinks is right, but there are instances where one needs to acknowledge the other's wrongs. It is not enough to always practice _pakikisama_. One must also weigh when and when not to stand up for what is right. In ethics, there are customs and traditions that are no longer relevant, like superstitious beliefs. As observed in the life of Rizal, he stood up for what was right, fought and struggled with it, and ended up dying for it. One needs to make up his or her mind whether what he or she thinks of and stands up for is right, and if it is right to preach or practice one's thoughts.

The _bahala na_ attitude comes from the term _Bathala_, the highest diety of the Tagalogs before the dawn of Christianity. _Bahala na_ literally translates to letting God's, or fate's, will be done. To say _bahala na_ without action would be wrong and futile. For example, a student has exams tomorrow and he chooses not to study as he is surrendering everything to the will of the Divine. Poverty is another issue. There are poor people who exclaim _bahala na_ without hardwork or without even trying to better the situation. Seeing _bahala na_ on a good lens, if one does everything, he could with all that he had, and then later on surrenders everything to fate or to the divine the things beyond his control. It is not wrong to pray and hope for the best, but isn't it that the answer to one's prayers shall be given through the abilities granted to him by God? Humans were given reason, ability, talent, and it is better we put them into good use.

Another common trait or expression is the Tagalog term _kasi_, which literally translates to "because". This portrays the Filipino tendency to find reason behind or mere excuse regarding one's errors instead of accountability. It is not wrong to come up with reasons for one's actions, especially those whose consequences he cannot foresee; this must be used to correct one's errors to avoid committing the same mistakes. What is wrong is if one only comes up with excuses and instead of showing accountability & responsibility and making up for what one did wrong, you end up blaming others for your mistakes. It is important to acknowledge one's shortcomings. This goes both on a personal and on a societal level, especially with regard to poverty, peace and security, and politics.

Close family ties is a very common Filipino trait. This illustrates one's sense of appreciation for the family knits or kinship. This is one of the reasons why in the Philippines, people of legal age still choose to live with and depend on the family. Even some married couples still stay with their parents. This is common in the Filipino household. This is good since members can look after each other, especially for those of old and very young age. This is why retirement homes and daycare centers are not common. What is also great about this is that good manners can definitely start at home. Close family ties means having more people to lean closely against, especially in cases of emergency. The sense of being together could also be priceless. However, the problem lies in members being heavily reliant or dependent. Because they share common homes, some no longer work hard to earn a living. Others often have misunderstandings and other forms of conflict within the household that never gets resolved.

Another Filipino trait which is illustrated in the common childhood tale: there was once a child who was asked by his mother to construct a stake for the banana tree trunk as it was about to fall over. The child responded with, "I'll do it tomorrow". At night, the winds blew harder, and the tree fell, and the fruit rotted. Today's youth has the common expression, "wait sa", everytime a task is given. Procrastination, known locally as the _mañana_ habit shows the Filipino tendency to postpone or put things for later. The word _mañana_ is a Spanish word which means "tomorrow". This is evident in the Filipino, on both personal and societal levels. But it is not inherently wrong to "put off till tomorrow what one can do today", especially if some of today's tasks require more importance and immediate attention. What is wrong is procrastinating even in the absence of any valid reason.

One Filipino trait, commonly a subject of Filipino philosophizing, is the abstract concept of _utang na loob_. This trait depicts the Filipino tendency to return the good favor, even when not asked to. This is one of the good reasons why most Filipinos take care of the elderly, not merely out of obligation, but out of gratitude for giving life. But sometimes, this goes the wrong way, because one may end up covering for the other's errors out of gratitude. In other instances, some family members do favors for the sake of family ties and end up getting abused. In politics, cronyism, or appointing friends to positions of authority regardless of qualification. The common reason would be _pakikisama_ or even _utang na loob_.

Lastly, the Filipino trait of _kanya-kanya_. This term, literally translating to, "to each his own", illustrates the tendency to put the self or the family first, over another, no matter the circumstance. A concrete example would be the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic. People were asked to stay home during the lockdowns, but there were those who did not strictly abide by the community quarantine. Their argument went along the lines of, if they would not leave the house to earn a living, then they would have nothing to sustain their needs with. All they cared about was how to make their families' ends meet, even if it meant going against the pandemic protocol. To put oneself first is not inherently wrong, but if one puts utmost importance in the self, this dissolves the idea of fraternity, even economic collapse and other societal impact. In the example aforementioned, even if one puts the self or the family first, the virus could get easily transmitted the more people go against the protocols, social and economic relations would still collapse, and a lot may still have lost their jobs. If such is the case, perhaps one may as well come up with a better solution to recover from the "horns of the dilemma."

Such are the 10 common Filipino traits. According to Professor Quito and Fr. Bulatao, one can speak about each's pros and cons. Even if not everyone possesses all of the traits, they can still be considered common as they are shared by most. But, as Prof. Quito and Fr. Bulatao emphasized, such traits can never be denied. Instead of denial, one must live with the good aspects of such traits and reject the evil.

## **INDIGENOUS DISPLACEMENT**

The early history of the Philippines recorded the arrival of the early inhabitants coming from different descents. If seen through the lens of Beyer, the first who arrived were the Dawn Men who were inherently hunters and fishers, but since the resources ran out, they left and settled on other places. This can be considered the first indigenous displacement, whose reason was not human induced, but inflicted by nature. The next to arrive were the Negritos, followed by the Indones. If the Indones stole the lands from the Negritos, no one could tell. The theory on the 10 Bornean Datus displacing the Negritos causing the latter to move to the highlands was merely a tale, contrary to what is now taught. Their cultural difference may not be a reason for territorial conflict, since the Negritos lived on hunting and fishing, while the Indones lived on gathering (agriculture). The vastness of the lands must also be considered, which is why the territorial conflict was deemed an impossible reason for displacement. If ever displacement transpired, this was because of natural causes such as the eruption of Mt. Pinatubo in 1991 which became the primary reason for the dispersing of the Negritos from the places they considered home for over millennia. Then came the Malays, and with them their advanced culture. Most of the Malays dwelled in the riverside because of trade and because of where the lands are more fit for cultivation. There was a big chance that the Malay farmers displaced the Indones farmers, and according to some historians, the Indones that Beyer wrote about were actually similar to the Austronesians who came from southwest Asia and the Pacific islands. This argument can be seen in the lens of William Henry Scott. What left a mark in history was the culture of the Malays which became the dominant culture of the islands before the Spaniards came. But there are a lot of possible explanations as to why the Indones and Malay farmers were only a singular unit, or why the Malay culture became dominant only because of their population, or if they became dominant only because they were more culturally-advanced and so all other factions ended up assimilating with them.

When the Spaniards came, there was physical indigenous displacement, which refers to the Malays. The clear historical account was that of the Spaniards forcefully banishing Lakan Dula, Rajah Mutanda, and Rajah Sulayman. And, because of evangelization instilled by the friars, including the development and progress brought by Legazpi from Spain, indigenous culture took a major turn, save for the Negritos who were mostly beyond friar reach, and the Muslims who struggled and fought against the Spaniards.

With the arrival of other foreigners like the Chinese, British, Americans, and Japanese, they brought with them their culture and tradition. In the passage of time, such cultures blended together that until today it is difficult to point out which was originally Malay. Even the Negritos were slowly adapting to the culture of the people from the lowlands, including the Muslims whose culture came from the West.

Hence, did indigenous displacement really take place? Probably. But the "original" or base culture was not completely banished, but only assimilated. One must think of culture not as something solid that needed crumbling or disposing for replacement. In contrast, culture is fluid and flowing, and may accommodate other cultures without straying away from the "original". Which is why if one claims that Filipino culture is the culture of the peoples before the foreigners arrived, we could disagree. Inasmuch as the Filipinos cannot take the blood of the Chinese or British or Spanish or Japanese, we cannot take the cultural influence of the foreigners away from our current culture.

As for physical displacement, the question that needed addressing was, who owned the ancestral lands and to what extent \- concepts that were not part of the indigenous vocabulary during the ancient times. Even today, a lot of indigenous peoples believe that people cannot own the land, for the land owns the people, for even when people perish, the land remains.
