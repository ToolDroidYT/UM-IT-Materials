# **CASTS AND MOULDS: ORIGINS OF THE MAN AND HIS NATIONALISM**

### **LEARNING OUTCOMES**

At the end of this section, students are expected to:

- understand Jose Rizal in the context of his times;
- analyze Rizal's family, childhood, and early education;
- evaluate the people and events and their influence on Rizal's early life; and
- examine the values highlighted by the various representations of Rizal as a national symbol.

At this point, the context of Rizal's nationalism shall be recounted, starting from examining his ancestry, social status, liberalizing hereditary influence, up to the harrowing events he experienced, including his sojourn into the realm of education.

## **ANCESTRY AND SOCIAL STATUS**

By ancestry, Rizal was a tornatras. The man known as "Pride of the Malay Race" also counted among his ancestors people of Chinese and Spanish descent. His paternal lineage can be traced to a couple named Siang-co and Zun-nio, from Sionque in the Chinchew District of Fujian Province in China. They had a son named Lam-co (esquire) who migrated to the Philippines around the late 1600s. Lam-co was baptized a Catholic in 1697, and adopted Domingo as his first name. He later on married the Chinese-mestiza daughter of Agustin Chin-co and Jacinta Rafaela of Parian named Ines de la Rosa. The couple, largely because of Domingo's close association with some friars, settled in San Isidro Labrador, an estate in Laguna owned by the Dominicans. They lived there along with other Chinese immigrants.

In 1731, Ines gave birth to a son who they named Francisco Mercado. The name Francisco was chosen in honor of the two friars who mattered to the family, while the surname Mercado (market) was in view of most ethnic Chinese being merchants. Francisco, in 1771, married a Chinese mestiza named Cirila Bernarda Monicha. She was originally from the \[Page 57\] nearby Augustinian-owned Hacienda San Pedro Tunasan which, like Biñan, was back then also home to many Chinese immigrants and sangleys. Orphaned quite early, she later went and lived with her family in Biñan. Francisco and Bernarda had a son named Juan. Following the mold of his father and grandfather, Juan married a Chinese mestiza named Cirila Alejandra who hailed from his grandfather's native Tubigan. She was, in fact, the daughter of Domingo's godson Juan Siong-co. The couple had 13 children, one of whom was Francisco Engracio.

Rizal's maternal lineage can be traced to the last ruler of the pre-Spanish kingdom of Tondo \- the great Sibunao Lacandola. Somewhere down the genealogy, there was a certain Captain Mariano Alejandro who bore to Faustina Florentina, a Chinese mestiza, a daughter named Maria Alejandro. Maria Alejandro was married to Captain Cipriano Alonzo, the son of a Chinese mestizo named Captain Gregorio Alonzo. Captain Cipriano and Maria had a son named Lorenzo Alberto Alonzo who, records described, was "very Chinese in appearance."

Lorenzo Alberto would marry a certain Brigida de Quintos and fathered with her Rizal's mother Teodora. Brigida de Quintos was of mixed ancestry. Historical records traced her maternal lineage to a certain Policarpio Ochoa. This employee of the Spanish Customs House had a son named Estanislao Manuel Ochoa who had a mix of Chinese-Tagalog-Castilian lineage. Among the family of Estanislao Manuel was a woman of such beauty that she was known as the "Sampaguita of the Parian." The woman, Mariquita Ochoa, managed to capture the eyes and heart of the exile known as the Marquis de Canete. Their union bore Regina Ochoa who, in turn, bore Brigida de Quintos to the lawyer Manuel de Quintos. Some other sources have Eugenio Ursua, a man of Japanese descent, as the father of Regina. Records agree, however, that Regina Ochoa was of Chinese-Tagalog-Spanish ancestry.

What can one learn from Rizal's colorful ancestry? Historians, Zaide in particular, argued that the mixed lineage that Rizal inherited helped him become who he became in the annals of Philippine history. Zaide noted that the Malayan blood flowing through his veins made Rizal the lover of freedom and of travel, as well as the man of unshakeable courage that he was. The same historian noted that his Chinese ancestry made him become the serious, patient, loving and frugal man that people know, and that his sensitivity to insult, elegance of bearing and gallantry to ladies he inherited from his Spanish lineage. More than these, however, what may concern the nation presently is knowing for whom Rizal was fighting. Was he fighting for the Chinese? \[Page 58\] Or the Spaniards? Or the Malays? Or the tornatras like him? As it turned out, his fight was not so much about ethno-cultural nationalism as it was about socio-political nationalism, that is to say, his nationalism did not look into lineages except as sources of good qualities or avenues for constructive criticisms.

As for his social status, Rizal was an indio, a principalia and an ilustrado. Although at some point in his recent past, Rizal's family suffered injustices at the hands of the Spaniards, the long genealogy from both the maternal and paternal sides boasted some of the who's who in the society. Rizal's paternal great-great-grandfather was a man of great social stature. He was a merchant who turned to farming because of the prejudice against "travelling merchants"—the sangleys—back then. After all, Sionque, his old hometown, was an agricultural community, and in China the most honorable employment is being a farmer. From the fact that Chinchew, the district under which Sionque belonged, was a literary center, he was presumed to be of the educated class. This presumption was reinforced by the fact that he married the daughter of an educated Chinese who was half his age and that, back when arranged marriages were the norm and being educated was the Chinese measure of social status, such union would have not been possible unless he was also an educated person.

He befriended well-known figures among the Dominicans who were instrumental in his settling in the Dominican estate in Biñan. He was a leader to the other Chinese immigrants who, like him, settled in Biñan. He was even called to Manila just to be a godfather. All these point to his status in the social hierarchy back then. Even his parents, Siang-co and Zun-nio, were presumed to be of higher social status given that the "-co" and "-nio" were titles akin to the Spanish "don" and "doña".

Ines de la Rosa Chinco, his wife, was also from a well-respected family. Her father, Agustin Chinco, was an educated man and a rice merchant, and her mother, Jacinta Rafaela was a daughter of a Christian Chinese. Domingo and Ines' son, Rizal's great grandfather, Francisco Mercado was also a man of great social stature. He was rich; he owned one of the largest herds of carabaos per the records preserved in the Biñan church. He was a local leader, being elected as the capitan del pueblo of Biñan in 1783\. He was popular; his name was often found in baptismal and wedding registries of Biñan. His wife, Bernarda, must have also been a popular godmother given that the name Monicha frequently formed part of the last names of girls around the area back then. \[Page 59\] Juan Francisco, Rizal's grandfather, was himself a man of political influence. He held the office of gobernadorcillo thrice, in 1808, 1813 and 1823\. In such capacity, he was one of the electors who eventually chose the insular Ventura de los Reyes to represent the Philippines to the Spanish Cortes in Cadiz. Capitan Juan proved to be a good and popular gobernadorcillo, he was noted to be generous and honest as a public servant, and there was no record of oppression during his terms which was uncharacteristic of officials back then. If there is one thing that could be associated with corruption in what he did, it was when he changed the status of his family from "Chinese mestizo" to "indio" thereby lowering the tribute that was due to the crown. But this, Craig explained, was to compensate for the tribute he paid on behalf of those who died or left Biñan during his term. Mrs. Mercado, Cirila Alejandro, was also of a high social standing and, like her husband, was generous, popular and well-liked by the people. Francisco Engracio, one of Juan's children and Rizal's father, was able to get an education despite being one of thirteen children. He later settled in Kalamba and became the tenant of the friar estate there. His success in farming enabled him to purchase properties from less successful farmers.

As prestigious and influential Rizal's paternal lineage was, his maternal ancestry was equally or even more prestigious and influential. Even if the royal blood of Lacandula were not to be counted, Teodora Alonzo's lineage could very well boast of men and women of high status in their social hierarchy. Estanislao Manuel Ochoa, an ancestor from her maternal side, was part owner of the Hacienda San Francisco de Malabon. He was of Castilian blood as was, presumably, his father Policarpio. And if one version of the story is to be believed, Teodora had in her veins flowing the noble blood of the Marquis de Cañete. Teodora's maternal grandfather, Manuel de Quintos, was a lawyer during the time when lawyers were very scarce and well-respected. His father Manuel, Teodora's great grandfather, was a municipal captain of Lingayen. Guerrero noted that Teodora's father, Lorenzo Alberto Alonzo, was a deputy of the Philippines to the Spanish Cortes, although the same author was cited by Ambeth Ocampo in his 2010 PDI article "Secrets locked in Alberto house about Rizal's mother" as saying that his search in Spain's records of deputies to the Spanish Cortes there yielded no mention of Lorenzo Alberto or Jose Alberto Alonzo. Historical records, however, provided with certainty that Lorenzo Alberto was the municipal captain of Biñan in 1824\. His father Cipriano and his grandfather Gregorio also held the position of capitan municipal of Biñan. Maria Florentina, Lorenzo Alberto's \[Page 60\] mother and Teodora's grandmother, belonged to the famous Florentina family of Bulacan. Her father, Mariano Alejandro, was also a municipal captain of Biñan. Quite a number of Teodora's other relatives were also professionals back when men of profession were rather few.

Now, if there is one thing one can learn for certain from the preceding presentation, it is the fact that Rizal was endowed with what the philosopher Immanuel Kant called the "gifts of fortune" \- he was fortunate to have been born to an economically and politically influential clan. The visits that his politically powerful ancestors had from Spanish dignitaries from Manila and even from the British kept them abreast with what was happening in the capital and even outside the country. The connection with the friars and the considerable influence they wielded over their communities cemented their legacy as principales, and in turn made the injustices some in their number later on suffered from the hands of the Spaniards more painful to bear. Their putting premium on education, and their capability to attain one, made Rizal the educated one that he became. These lead to the question: had Rizal been born otherwise, would he have even desired for the betterment of the country? Would he have been able, granted that he indeed had desired, to do what he did for the country?

## **LIBERALIZING HEREDITARY INFLUENCE**

At this point, the stories of events that happened even before Rizal was born will be narrated \- stories that have helped shaped Rizal's worldview and nationalism through what the American biographer and historian James Alexander Robertson mentioned, in his introduction of Craig's "Lineage, Life and Labors of Jose Rizal, Philippine Patriot", selective principle.

It must be noted that Rizal's desire was not freedom from Spain but freedom from the Spanish colonial government that failed to bring the Islands to a status greater than the "Medieval Europe" that the early colonizers gave to the Philippines. This seemingly natural response to the stifling of the march of progress brought about by tyrannical rule and suppression of liberty and equality echoed the experiences and sentiments of his Chinese ancestors under the Manchus. His preference for the liberation of the minds as the means towards true liberty was, likewise, in all appearances inherited from them. His love-hate feeling towards Spain might have as well been brought about by the long history of benevolence and persecution suffered by the Chinese under the Spanish colonial government. \[Page 61\] The Chinese, Domingo Lam-co being one of them, found favor from the Dominicans in the form of them being taken in by the Order during the time when there was an imminent threat of another massacre. This is not to say that the Order did so solely for pious reasons, it benefited greatly from the services of these industrious farmers after all. But while the friars gained reliable tenants, the Chinese were as delighted as being able to live in security, and practice what China considered the noblest profession. Under the auspices of the Dominicans, the Chinese, Domingo Lam-co in particular, thrived in the Order's estate in Biñan. Such good fortune was inherited down the line even by Francisco Engracio. But the story was not at all a purely happy one; there were episodes of disagreement and even of persecution. A tradition in the family of Francisco, Lam-co's son, for one notes of a "tedious and costly lawsuit" filed against the Dominicans. Joaquin de Quintos, the brother of Rizal's maternal great-great grandfather, also once led a complaint filed against the "arbitrariness" of their provincial governor. These and a couple more incidents much more personal to Rizal detailed in the succeeding texts are but some of the stories of disagreement and persecution recorded in history.

Disputes over the land, as was the case with Francisco's lawsuit, was then known as a fairly common issue. As Craig noted, "History has recorded enough agrarian trouble, in all ages and in all countries, to prove the economic mistake of large holdings of land by those who do not cultivate it." The story of Francisco's lawsuit was that the tenants were asked to pay an increased rent. Considering that the estate improved due to the industry that the tenants poured into, it was but natural for them to protest. A sympathetic Spanish judge, upon reviewing the deeds and measuring the estate, decided that the landlords were at fault. The appeals court, however, overturned the decision. And such was considered an injustice suffered by the weak at the hands of the powerful \- an injustice that was still well remembered during Rizal's time.

There was also this issue about personal liberty and rights of nations. England (and the subsequent United Kingdom), which has a long list of laws safeguarding individual liberties even back when monarchs tended to do otherwise, became the standard of good governance. In Spain the number of liberals, so-called because of their campaign for liberties guaranteed to individual citizens, began growing. And when freemasonry was reintroduced in the country, a lot of these liberals became freemasons. Unfortunately, both their cause and affiliation brought them at odds \[Page 62\] with the Church and the State. When the Bourbon war against England happened, these liberals were accused of being traitors. And with Pope Clement XII's _In Eminente Apostolatus Specula_ condemning Freemasonry, things went further downhill for them. Fortunately, with the intercession of the Queen, King Philip V commuted the sentence from death to banishment. And it was thus that these liberal minded officials, as they were later delegated to be in the colonies, brought modern ideas to Mexico, Cuba and the Philippines. The arrival of these new breed of officers, plus the already existing cajas abiertas (lit. open box) \- Spanish of noble birth but temporarily went out of the throne's favor and were known for their prestige and generosity \- made the people in the Islands realize that there were better Spaniards than the ones running the colony. These events were personally known to Francisco Mercado.

When the British briefly occupied Manila from 1762-1764, and when the then Lieutenant Governor of Manila Simon Anda y Salazar was waging guerilla warfare from Bulacan, another liberalizing experience was made known to Francisco Mercado and his contemporaries. Biñan went under the influence of the British, and through them Filipinos realized that freer trade is much better than the one espoused by the Spaniards and that their judicial system was definitely better than that of the Spanish colonial government.

Because the Chinese sided with the British, Anda's campaign counted among its casualties some 6000 Chinese. Then came the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1768 which, in effect, took the only source of knowledge for some about Spain and about the Church. But Biñan continued enjoying the benefits of being a well-traveled place. The people got to get a broader look at things.

The expulsion of the Jesuits became the center point of other interconnected events, the lingering effects of which were felt even during the time of Rizal. For one, Archbishop Don Basilio Sancho de Santa Justa y Rufina, who was one of the most active enforcers of King Charles III's expulsion of the Jesuits became the Archbishop of Manila in 1766 (J.A. Le Roy, 1914, The Americans in the Philippines Vol. 1, p.20). Part of the task given to him by the King was to check on the regular clergy (Dominicans, Augustinians, etc.) for possible abuses. Being a secular, he implemented his task with gusto, especially that he secured the cooperation of the then Governor General Anda who still harbored resentment against the friars because he was denounced a usurper by Archbishop Rojo back during the British occupation. Among the reforms he instituted was uniform charges for the rites of the Church and \[Page 63\] putting secular clergy in-charge of parishes. Both of these brought him in conflict with the Augustinians and the Dominicans.

The expulsion of the Jesuits also had a lasting impact in the Dominican Estate Hacienda de Kalamba. The Estate was formerly owned by the Jesuits. When the Jesuits were expelled, the government confiscated the land. To the common people, the act of the government was short of being sacrilegious considering that the money with which the lands were bought was donated for the propagation of the faith. (Aguilar, Felomeno, 2017 Colonial sugar production in the Spanish Philippines: Calamba and Negros compared, Journal of Southeast Asian Studies, Vol. 48 Issue 2, pp.237-261, p.)

The confiscation of the Kalamba Estate by the government reinforced the knowledge that there was a dissension between the Church and the government; there was even before the time of Governor General Manuel de Bustamante who was assassinated at the behest of the friars and the Jesuits because of his eagerness to implement the King's desire to put up a secular university in the Islands (Le Roy). And with a secular clergy at the helm of this new secularization, there revealed a dissent even inside the Church. Such dissents, especially that within the Church, destroyed the illusion of oneness and inseparability which was the foundation of Spain's power in the colony. And these things were carefully observed and noted by, among others, Francisco Mercado.

A year after the death of Francisco, the government sold the estate to a lay Spaniard Don Jose Clemente de Azanza. When de Azanza died in 1833, the estate was acquired by the Dominicans. It was from the Dominicans that Francisco Engracio leased the land they cultivated when they migrated from Biñan to Kalamba.

During the youth of Juan and Clemente, England was embroiled in a war with its former colony the United States. News of the American War for Independence reached the Philippines through the Spaniards themselves. Because of the resentment they held against Great Britain, they encouraged the circulation of any news favorable to the Americans. Through it, the Filipinos realized that the injustices done to the Americans, and for which they demanded redress that they were willing to die for, were trivial compared to the injustices done to the Filipinos.

Another bloodier war was indirectly brought about by the American Revolution. France's state coffers were drained as a result of its interference in the war being waged by the American colonies against the United Kingdom. The King taxed everyone \- the poor and the noblemen alike \- in order to procure government funds. All of these \[Page 64\] happened when France was also experiencing famine. As civilian riots broke out and the looming prospect of military takeover loomed in the background, confusion, terror, and violence swept the nation's capital. The French stormed the Bastille in 1793, which historians regard to be the beginning of the French Revolution, just six years after the American Revolution came to an end in 1783\. France was thrusted into a reign of terror when Louis XVI and Marie-Antoinette were executed by the Jacobin leader Maximilien Robespierre; what was initially relatively mild revolution quickly became bloodier.

In contrast to the French Revolution which proved nothing but the terror that a revolution of \- in Craig's words \- the "ignorant and oppressed" would bring, the American Revolution was applauded. This was partially because Spain showered praise upon the Americans in order to infuriate the United Kingdom. This demonstrated to the Filipino that genuine freedom could only be attained by the intellectual; as a result, to travel the world in pursuit of education was deemed necessary, if education at home is less feasible. For what it's worth, Juan baptized the boy Ferdinand, when Napoleon sought to persuade the Filipinos to recognize Joseph Bonaparte as king.

The educational system in the Philippines at that time called for radical change. The slaughter of foreigners in Manila and Cavite in 1819 (Foreman 1906 dated it 1820; see also Craig) was a stark illustration of the profound ignorance that this type of education had left the Filipinos with. The mistaken notion of the locals that a French naturalist's collection of animals was to blame for the deadly Asian cholera led to the mass killing. As a form of compensation after everything was said and done, the foreigners only obtained less restrictions and safer travel and trade. The news that Spain had sold Florida to the United States that year then reached the Philippines. This dispelled the myth that Spain keeps the integrity of all her dominions in high regard; Spain would be willing to give up her colonies, given the right amount of pressure.

Spain was experiencing political unrest during the time Juan was the alcalde of Biñan. In 1808, Ferdinand VII \- less than two months into his reign was compelled to resign by Napoleon Bonaparte, his former ally against Portugal and against his father Charles IV, in favor of his brother Joseph. Napoleon imprisoned Ferdinand and crowned Joseph King of Spain. The Spanish people disagreed, but the government did. Revolts broke out, and juntas, who rendered Joseph's administration invalid, were established. The liberals in Spain first supported the French; it dawned on them \[Page 65\] later on, however, that the French Revolution's aims had not been accomplished. They later rose to prominence inside the Junta de Seville, which triumphed over the other juntas and played a crucial role in the drafting of the Spanish Constitution of 1812\. Napoleon's defeat by the Russians and Joseph's by the Duke of Wellington, which resulted in Napoleon's abdication and imprisonment on the island of Elba, led to Ferdinand's triumphal return as king in 1814\.

Contrary to the expectation of the liberals, Ferdinand did not honor the Cadiz Constitution and instead fashioned himself as an absolute monarch. This royal act contributed to the declaration of independence of what remained of the Spanish colonies in the Americas. Then in 1820, the el Trienio Liberal began. The King was forced to accept the Constitution of Cadiz after the successful revolt led by Colonel Rafael del Riego y Nuñez. While Ferdinand remained the head of state, he was but a prisoner of the Cortes. Then in 1823, the French Louis XVIII under the authority of the Concert of Europe sent an army to Spain to save Ferdinand from the Cortes. The first thing that Ferdinand did was to capture and exile the liberals. All these meant one significant thing to the Philippines in particular: the representation to the Cortes guaranteed by the Cadiz Constitution did not have any meaning at all, for most of the time the Island's representative would arrive in Spain only to find out that the Cortes was dissolved.

Around these times, the Iberian colonies in the New World had already achieved or were about to achieve their independence. One other thing that Ferdinand did upon resuming his rule was planning for the reconquest of Spain's lost colonies. Then in 1823, James Monroe issued what was later known as the Monroe Doctrine. The main message that this doctrine sent to all powers in the Old World was this: any act of aggression against the new independent countries would be considered an act of aggression against the United States of America. And this message planted in the minds of the Filipinos the corollary idea that the Philippines is for the Filipinos.

But, contrary to popular belief, the Spaniards in the Philippines were not pure evil. The Spanish Inquisition, which basically means death sentence in Europe, assumed a gentler face in the Islands where suspects were basically let off with merely a warning. There was no wholesale enslaving of the people like what happened in the Americas, for the Church, at the friars' behest, forbade such enslavement. The very first bishop of Manila himself, Domingo de Salazar, called a council and "threatened with hellfire those who thus betrayed Spain's sacred mission" (Guerrero). If there is \[Page 66\] one thing the friars were guilty of, it was self-preservation which, in Craig's view, though deplorable, were to be expected. The official policy adopted as institutions \- Church and government \- was one of self-defense and not revenge (see Guerrero, p.22). There were noted abuses by one or two friars and governors-general, but these were the fault of individuals and not of institutions.

But conflict was bound to happen when you have, on one side, people who happened to know about material progress, and on the other, the friars who were accustomed to tradition and the absoluteness of their Order's Rule. And, although the Filipinos dared not voice such sentiments, the Spaniards knew that the former were holding it. And when uprisings arose, innocent people suffered along with the guilty from both sides. Instead of reflecting on the mistakes and finding avenues for change, the people tend to go down the path of bloodshed.

All these were clear to Rizal. Like the characters Ibarra and Simoun in his novels who both loved and hated the Church, Rizal hated the abusive Spain. But he also loved the gentle Spain and waited until his death for that country to finish the task of bringing progress to the country started by Magellan, Legazpi, Urdaneta and Salazar. And all his life was spent convincing the mother country to look at the deplorable state to which the successors of Legazpi brought the Philippines. At the same time, as his message to Bonifacio and his correspondence with Marcelo del Pilar show he also counseled the Filipinos against taking the path of bloodshed.

And those were the stories. Perhaps, it would not be so much of an overstatement to say that the events mentioned—events that shaped the worldview and even character of his forebears—had profound impact in Rizal's own world views and characteristic response to similar incidences; that had events happened otherwise or had his ancestors been impacted by these events differently, there could have been a very real chance that Rizal would not have the worldview that he had or that he could not have reacted the way he did.

## **HARROWING EVENTS**

At this point, stories of liberalizing events that happened during Rizal's lifetime—events that likewise affected his worldview and nationalism profoundly—will be narrated.

Of all the incidents that happened in the personal life of Rizal, there were two that stood out and, in the minds of the biographer Leon Maria Guerrero, brought \[Page 67\] Rizal to the path that he would take: the execution of the three secular priests, and the injustices done to his mother.

Rizal himself said, as quoted by Guerrero, "Had it not been for the events of 1872, Rizal would have been a Jesuit." Such was the impact of the execution of Jose Burgos, Mariano Gomes and Jacinto Zamora to the life of Rizal. The experience was personal to Rizal in that Burgos had been a housemate of his elder brother Paciano Mercado. Moreover, that fateful incident on February 17, 1872 was seen by many not merely as an execution but a declaration of war against the secularization of the parishes and the growing liberal sentiments. Rizal would later dedicate his second, more radical novel to the three priests.

The GomBurZa, as the priests were later collectively called, had been a thorn on the sides of both the regular priests and the Spanish seculars ever since they took on the mantle left by Padre Pedro Pablo Pelaez. Although a full-blooded Spaniard \- his father was a peninsular and his mother was a creole of Spanish parents \- Padre Pelaez's sympathy in the conflict between the Spanish and the native seculars was with the latter.

The secularization controversy started almost a century prior when the secular Archbishop of Manila, Don Basilio Santa Justa decided to uphold and enforce the juridical right of the local ordinary over the parishes that belonged to his diocese. When the friars who ran most of these parishes resigned in protest, and when there were too many parishes for the Spanish seculars to handle, the Archbishop decided to ordain native clergies. And during the time of Padre Pelaez, aside from the conflict between the regular and the secular, there was also one between the Spanish and the native seculars.

Padre Mariano Gomes de los Angeles was Pelaez's co-leader in the seculars' protest against the order from Spain mandating secular priests to handover their parishes to the regulars (Blanco, 2010). They wrote a petition in the name of the Cavite seculars to the Queen to abolish the order and, at the same time, affirmed the seculars' loyalty. They also raised funds to maintain an agent in Madrid. His being a Chinese mestizo priest working with a creole one was seen as a more widespread and comprehensive action of the seculars.

Padre Jose Apolonio Burgos, an insular, was a protégé of Padre Pelaez (Uckung, 2012). An intellectual whose loyalty to the Church and the Crown was noticed and many times commended by then Manila Archbishop Gregorio Meliton Martinez, \[Page 68\] Padre Burgos was also a leader in the secularization movement after the death of Pelaez. The "pamphlet" published in 1864 containing a manifesto defending the honor of Pelaez against the baseless accusation that he led a movement to overthrow the colonial government and, at the same time, the rights of the secular clergy was generally attributed to him. Although the Jesuit historian Schumacher managed to identify that the authorship of the manifesto was not Burgos alone, he was able to establish that there indeed was a connecting line of nationalist thought from Pelaez to Burgos, Burgos to Paciano Mercado, and Paciano to Rizal (and even going beyond to Bonifacio and Jacinto) (Schumacher, 2006).

Padre Jacinto Zamora, based on his letter of application for the first tonsure, was a "mestizo español". Although a year older than Padre Burgos, he was considered a follower of the latter. His case was a classic example of being in the wrong place (or with the wrong people or having the wrong name) at the wrong time. Or was it? The orden de arresto served to him was supposed to be for a certain Jose Maria Zamora (probably the one that bested him and Burgos in the examination for vacant curacies in 1864). But for some ill stroke of luck, a note was found in his drawer bearing this message: "Gran reunion. Acude V. sin falta. Los amigos vendran provistos de balas y polvora." Upon finding the note, the serving officer simply crossed out Jose Maria's name and inserted Jacinto's. The fact that Padre Burgos was in his house taking care of another sick priest did not help his case. Or, perhaps, it was his addiction to gambling that finally did him in, for the incriminating note was simply talking about playing cards (bullets) and money (powder) (Arcilla 2012).

Perhaps the authorities had been looking for years for an excuse to finally pull these thorns from their sides. Then happened the Mutiny of 1872 almost a month before January 20\. Led by the Spanish mestizo Sgt. Fernando La Madrid, some 200 Filipino soldiers and laborers of Fort San Felipe in Cavite mutinied, killed at least the commander of the Fort (Schumacher 2011\) and took over. Two conflicting accounts as to what that bloody incident was really all about have survived to this day. The Spaniards, specifically the historian Jose Montero y Vidal and that time's incumbent Governor General Rafael Izquierdo, were convinced based on the results of their investigations that the revolution, as they called it, was part of a concerted effort to overthrow the Spanish colonial government (Piedad-Pugay 2012; Schumacher 2011). Trinidad Hemenegildo Pardo de Tavera, a researcher-historian nephew of one of those sentenced into exile in the aftermath of the incident, maintained that the \[Page 69\] bloody uprising was simply a mutiny of dissatisfied employees. Despite the conflicting versions, however, one thing remained certain: the Cavite Mutiny was a product of the proverbial changing of fortunes.

The quite lengthy account provided by LMG detailed this changing of fortunes. When the last Habsburg King of Spain Charles II died without an issue, the crown fell to the Bourbons of France. Mounting their own Age of Enlightenment, which was actually a form of "enlightened despotism", the Bourbons starting with Charles III introduced reforms designed to make the government more centralized as well as to make the colonies more developed for the benefit of the crown. The costly war against England to which Bonaparte dragged Spain resulted in the kingdom going bankrupt and the people extremely dissatisfied. The Spanish liberals, as mentioned earlier, waged their guerilla warfare, won through the help of the Duke of Wellington and established the Cadiz Constitution. One key element in that liberal constitution was the Cortes, the representation to which became one of the cornerstones of the reformist movement in the Philippines. When Bonaparte was defeated, and the French monarchy reinstituted, Ferdinand VII resumed his aborted reign. Ferdinand's attitude towards liberalism \- treating it with respect only as a matter of contingency \- seemed to have been inherited by his daughter Isabela who came into power, defeated his pretender uncle and was declared by the liberals only to be later on deposed by the same liberals. Francisco Serrano, one of the generals who led against Isabella, became the president of the provisional government, and at the same time prime minister and regent of the kingdom. He was the man who sent Carlos Maria de la Torre y Nava Cerrada to assume the office of Governor General of the Philippines.

Governor General de la Torre was no alien to the politics of his time. He was a rich man born in the Andalusian capital of Seville, a military man who managed to climb up to the rank of Lieutenant General and was a deputy to the Spanish Cortes. He was also a liberal to the core. He was part of the army that casted Isabella II away. He came to the Philippines with the intent of implementing the desires of the monarchical democracy that he himself helped to install \- the respect for the rights and freedoms of the inhabitants of the Islands who were citizens of Spain according to the Cadiz Constitution. One of the first things that he did as Governor General of the Philippines was to try to eradicate corruption both in the government and in the Church. He also gave reprieve to the freedom of expression by abolishing the censorship of the press and encouraging freedom of speech and assembly. In the \[Page 70\] army, instead of flogging, he instituted imprisonment as punishment for troublesome troops. He even pardoned the "rebels" of Cavite's agrarian revolt and made them members of the new group auxiliary to the Civil Guard. He made their leader Casimiro Camerino the captain of the corps. He also was sympathetic to the plight of the secular priests who were "dispossessed" of their parishes upon the return of the Jesuits in 1859\.

Although de la Torre's precautionary action of drawing a list of people with liberal ideas was instrumental in the execution or banishment of individuals during the aftermath of the mutiny of 1872, his was the most liberal government in a very long while. The insulars, the mestizos, the Indios \- they all welcomed the man and the atmosphere he created.

But such a honeymoon was never destined to last. Spain was still in political turmoil. General Serrano's government was but a regency while the Cortes Generales was looking for a suitable monarch. Soldiers might have taken over the reins, but the politicians were always hanging around. And it was even common to see both a soldier and a politician in one person. There were those who expressed their support to Isabella's son Alfonso. Others were reluctant thinking that the 11-year old Alfonso would be greatly influenced by his mother. And the Carlists, supporters of Isabella's uncle Charles, were as strong as ever. Finally, the Cortes decided to have the Italian Duke of Aosta, Amadeo, sit on the Spanish throne.

Amadeus' reign was the shortest in Spanish history. But a single act within such a short time managed to greatly alter the history of the Philippines. In 1871, King Amadeus appointed a decorated general as the Governor General of the Philippines. Rafael Geronimo Cayetano Izquierdo y Gutierrez was one of the liberals who effectively ended the reign of Isabella. Even in the Philippines, contrary to popular belief, he showed his liberal side (Arcilla, 2013). He did attempt at implementing the old order of secularizing the schools and forbidding friars to teach. He wanted to raise the salaries of teachers. He also insisted on opening industrial and technical schools. But the situation in the Philippines turned him less liberal \- a "conservative liberal" as he himself said. The national government coffers were empty, corruption was rampant and the threat of rebellion from unpacified territories loomed in the background. The shadow of Pedro Pelaez was also still palpable.

Perhaps these led him to take measures that, for those who experienced freer life under de la Torre, were stifling. The opening of a school for arts and trade was \[Page 71\] suspended. Mestizos de Español and Españoles Filipinos who held high positions both in the civil government and the military were stripped of their positions. The exemption to give tribute and render forced labor enjoyed particularly by the workers in the navy-yard, too, was abolished. And when the Cavite Mutiny broke out, he had to take drastic steps. After all, the "evidence" that he obtained were really pointing to an organized revolution aimed at bringing down the Spanish colonial government and not merely a mutiny of disgruntled dockyard employees. Being a veteran of a revolution himself, he must have known the repercussions of not instituting drastic responses to such actions. What he did might have been wrong and or excessive \- his trying civilians in a military court was condemned in Spain \- but he acted based on what he thought was right. Gomes was Pelaez' co-champion for secularization, Burgos was a protégé of Pelaez, and the ill-fated Zamora was caught with that incriminating note on his drawer.

Alas that the three were not masons, for an interesting footnote to the Cavite Mutiny was the exile of the GomBurZa's co-suspects who were incidentally masons. Had they been masons, perhaps they would also have ended up being exiled, the Bagumbayan executions \- that in 1872 and the other, more celebrated one 24 years later \- would not have happened. But fate had other plans; and thus, history was left with the three secular priests on top of the scaffold \- Gomez resigned to his fate, Zamora losing his mind and Burgos weeping like a child. Even the powerful Archbishop of Manila failed to do anything to prevent this execution.

But a contemporaneous, more personal incident happened around this time \- an event immortalized by the story of Sisa in the Noli. Rizal himself recounted this incident in his "anonymous student diary." No less dramatic than the story of Helen of Troy, the incident began with an unfaithful wife. Jose Alberto Alonzo, Doña Teodora's brother, returned home from a business trip in Europe only to find "his home deserted, his children abandoned" (LMG 14). He looked for his wife only to find out that she was "living with another man" (Zaide 25). He intended to divorce her, but Doña Teodora persuaded him not to. After the affair was settled, the Alonzos again lived together. Unknown to Doña Teodora, the unfaithful wife (Teodora Formoso) actually resented her for saving the marriage. Together with a lieutenant of the Guardia Civil, she accused her husband of trying to poison her; Doña Teodora was supposed to be his accomplice. The gobernadorcillo \- the judge of the case \- did not even bother to judiciously review the accusation and the evidence presented. \[Page 72\] Don Antonio Vivencio del Rosario immediately ordered for her to be taken to the provincial jail in the capital Sta. Cruz, and she was supposed to go the 50-kilometer distance on foot. Rizal also recounted her mother being forced to admit something in exchange for her freedom and the chance to be with her children (Leon Maria Guerrero 15).

Rizal's mother was incarcerated for two and a half years. She filed a charge of prejudice against the gobernadorcillo, with the assistance of Manila's "most renowned lawyers," Don Francisco de Marcaida and Don Manuel Marzano. She was released after the Royal Audiencia in Manila concluded that her contention was valid. Although the gobernadorcillo abided by the court decision, he claimed that Doña Teodora's accusation was a contempt of court, thus she was given a longer sentence (Craig 51). This time, the Audiencia sided with the jury, but since Doña Teodora had already been imprisoned for long, the Audiencia once more ordered for her release. She was charged with murder, theft, and cruelty. Had fate not been set up so that the visiting Governor General could see Soledad dancing (Uckung 2012), her case would have carried on. In a tale reminiscent of Herod and Salome, the Governor General asked Soledad for whatever favor she wished, to which she requested none other than her mother's freedom.

Austin Craig recognized the Spanish judicial injustice suffered by Doña Teodora as "the greatest influence" on the young Rizal. And he would remember it years later. Leon Maria Guerrero thought of it in the same line. Rizal might have dedicated the El Filibusterismo to the memory of the GomBurza. He might have, as his very own statement revealed, credited to the unjust execution of the three priests his chosen path: "Had it not been for the events of 1872, Rizal would have been a Jesuit." But, as noted by Guerrero (16), the injustice suffered by Doña Teodora made a profound impact in Rizal's life. A diary entry mentioned of him losing both his confidence in friendship and trust in his fellowmen because of the ill-fate suffered by his mother. Also, the more than a decade of intervening time between that faithful incident and Rizal's writing of his first novel neither dimmed the vividness nor assuaged the sorrow. Sisa's walk of shame, noted Guerrero, is but a fictional representation of his mother's.

Doña Teodora's suffering might not have been as widely renowned and as brutal as that of the GomBurZa, but it was more intimate and personal to Rizal. In fact it \[Page 73\] was so intimate that Rizal, as Guerrero noted, could not bear to talk about it except in a diary written by an adolescent and in a fiction work written by an adult.

And those were the stories that no longer in want of any elaboration. The frustration and the pain that Rizal felt, needless to say, might have been the last straws drawn before Rizal took the path he had taken.

At this point, the events pertaining to Rizal's education—events that, also contributed in shaping his nationalistic sentiments—shall be discussed.

## **EARLY EDUCATION**

Education has been an integral part of Rizal's system from even before he was born up until his death, and beyond. One thing that he inherited from his Chinese forebears through the so-called selective principle was his affinity for education which was, as elsewhere noted, for the Chinese an indication of a high status in the society. But more than the social status, the Chinese also believed that true reforms can only come through education \- that a man who wishes reforms for his country must also transform himself so that he will be worthy of a reformed country. And this Rizal imbibed until his death.

Like any ordinary boy, Rizal's sojourn into the wide realm of education started within the cozy confines of that stone house in Kalamba. People around helped mold his young, inquisitive mind. His aya read him stories. He usually borrowed notes and lessons from an older sister. His Tio Jose Alberto helped him develop his artistic talent. Another Tio, the "giant" Manuel, was instrumental in transforming Rizal's sickly body into a suppler one through exercises, wrestling and other physical activities. A third, Tio Gregorio, instilled in him some basic principles in life: the reality that the world would give nothing of value for free, that one should finish the work started as rapidly as possible, and that thinking for oneself would be way better than merely following what others would dictate (Craig 41). Even the parish priest of the nearby Church was part of the young boy's education. Padre Leoncio Lopez was instrumental in the growth of Rizal's "love for scholarship and intellectual honesty" (Zaide 19).

But of all the influences in the young child's mind, nothing could beat that of his parents. Doña Teodora was Rizal's first teacher. She taught him how to read so at three Rizal already knew his letters. But it was not without a great deal of patience on her part. In one particular reading session, he recalled, her mother got so impatient \[Page 74\] listening to him read poorly that she took the book and started reading it out loud for him. To his defense, he later would write, he blamed his lack of enthusiasm to the fact that the book concerned was the Spanish translation of _The Children's Friend_ by Abbe Sabatier. "I did not understand Spanish and so could not put any expression into my reading", he would say. It was from that book that the classic story of the moth was taken \- a story that somehow prefigured what Rizal would do years later. Like the foolish young moth that burned itself on the light that so attracted it, Rizal would die because he could not resist the lure of education. But, of course, Rizal was never foolish.

The Rizal family also enlisted the help of private tutors: Maestro Celestino, Maestro Lucas Padua and Maestro Leon Monroy. Unfortunately, not much is known about these three beyond their names and the fact that the third, who tutored Rizal in Spanish and Latin, was a former classmate of Don Francisco Engracio.

Since education is not only about academics, it must also be noted that Doña Teodora taught Rizal the basic prayers. In his student diary he fondly recalled the prayers that he learned to say and "raise fervently to God". Don Francisco Engracio, though not as visible as his wife where Rizal's early education at home is concerned, was nonetheless a teacher of a different sort. His influence in Rizal's education went beyond his famed insistence to send Rizal to Biñan years later. In his life and works years later one could still find the lessons in life that Rizal learned from the simple policies that his father instituted, like encouraging Rizal to alternate walking with his dog Usman and riding on his pony while touring the countryside, and his allowing Rizal to spend the night in the huts where farmers stay and share myths and legends.

When Maestro Monroy died, Don Francisco Engracio, himself once educated in Biñan, decided against the protestation of his wife to send Rizal to a school there. He was nine years old when he, accompanied by Paciano, left home. His teacher at Biñan, Maestro Justiniano Aquino Cruz, was "of the old school" as Craig described him. He was famous for effectively giving flesh to such sayings as "Spare the rod, spoil the child" and "The letters enter with blood." Rizal's description of him years later hinted that he was not overly fond of this teacher. "He was a tall, thin man with a long neck and a sharp nose and a body slightly bent forward" and a severity that was exaggerated, he would write. His distaste for Maestro Cruz' means would later figure prominently in the _Noli Me Tangere_: Ibarra planned to build a school that "would not be a torture chamber but a playground of the mind." \[Page 75\] Not much is known about Rizal's academic training in that Biñan School except that instructions in Spanish and Latin grammar were part of it, presumably since Maestro Cruz was an expert on these, and the fact that he excelled in his academics. He became so good that it took him only a few months before Maestro Cruz declared that he already knew as much as his teacher, and that it would be better for him to go to Manila for further education.

Life in Biñan was not all about academics. There were painting lessons under an old painter named Juancho which further honed his artistic talent. There were also incidents that would remind us that Rizal was as human as anyone. He had a brawl with Maestro Cruz' son and arm-wrestling with another classmate. He also frequently played in the streets together with his nieces.

All in all, his stay in Biñan was a fruitful if not a happy one. The homesickness that he felt the very night of his arrival in Biñan, being an “errand boy" of his aunt that he so detested and, of course, the beatings that he suffered under Maestro Cruz \- these were the darker parts of his Biñan stay. But he got to live and spend a part of his life in his ancestors' place. He met with relatives from whom he learned much about his paternal lineage. And it was there that he first heard of Morga, whose book he would later dedicate a part of his life to annotate, and Sir John Bowring, the notable polyglot whose achievement because of his mastery of the languages Rizal wanted to rival.

On December 17, 1870, he left Biñan for Manila aboard the steamer Talim. Indeed, his stay in his ancestors' place was bitter-sweet that Zaide described Rizal's leaving it as a sad experience \- "He prayed in the town church, collected pebbles in the river for souvenirs, and regretfully bade farewell to his teacher and classmates," Zaide wrote while Guerrero noted that Rizal anticipated it with immense happiness because aside from his first ride aboard a steamship and meeting again his beloved family he would then be free from the beatings of Maestro Cruz and the errands of his aunt.

Barely two years after he left Biñan, the Cavite Mutiny happened. One notable result, as was elsewhere noted, was the execution of GomBurZa. The three priests, or at least Gomez and Burgos, were known for their efforts in the Secularization Movement. It would then be totally understandable for people to suspect the friars as the masterminds of the execution.

The execution of the GomBurZa, or to be exact the supposed reason for it, had impacted not just Rizal's family but also his education. As Guerrero noted, back then \[Page 76\] there were only three secondary schools that fit best for Rizal in terms of intellectual and financial capability, and social connections \- all of them connected in some way to the Dominican Order. There was the Colegio de San Jose which, although originally under the supervision of the Jesuits, was from 1875 until 1910 incorporated into the University of Santo Tomas. There was also the Dominican owned and administered Colegio de San Juan de Letran. A third, the Ateneo Municipal de Manila, although administered by the Jesuits, was in some way under the Dominicans specifically with the latter exercising "certain powers of inspection and regulation."

The option to enroll at San Jose was practically closed to him. Paciano was a student there. His connection with Padre Burgos earned him the ire of the colonial government. As Craig noted, he "regularly failed in passing his examinations because of his outspokenness against the evils of the country." Letran, perhaps because of its being owned and administered by the Dominicans, was also not an option. In any case, Rizal was perhaps destined to study under the Jesuits. But even his entry to the Ateneo was not without some difficulty. He had to take the entrance exam at the Letran and, although he received a passing mark in Christian doctrine, reading and arithmetic (the subjects of the examination), he still was initially denied entry to the Ateneo because of his poor health and short stature, and the fact that the term had already started. In the end, Paciano had to secure the help of Padre Burgos' nephew Manuel Xerez to secure Rizal's entry to Ateneo.

It was in Ateneo that he earned the scholastic renown that people often associate with him. Although, as some serious historians would as often point out, such scholastic record was not exactly super special; it was achieved before Rizal, it was achieved hence. But for certain, it was special, especially considering that, as Guerrero noted, the Jesuit curriculum for the Bachelor of Arts was six-years long and was more difficult than today's. To be graded excellent in all subjects of that curriculum must be something special. Also, Rizal's path to that scholastic greatness did not actually start smoothly. Although he was good right off the start \- and had been consistently so \- he was initially not the award-harvesting student people often imagine him to be. His relatively average start was blamed on his sensitivity (he resented a remark given to him by a professor which led him to stop taking studies seriously), to the 25-minute walk from his original boarding house to the school, his poor Spanish, and the distractions at Doña Pepay's boarding house. It was not until he boarded at the Ateneo that he started to truly excel. Taking his relatively average performance \[Page 77\] the previous year as motivation, he noted that "I was almost thirteen years old, and I had yet to gain a reputation for brilliance among my classmates" \- he started to give more focus on his studies again. This devotion to study was reciprocated, if not more, by the world-renowned Jesuit education which is anchored on _ratio studiorum_ (reasons/plans of studies), the emphasis of which is described as "rather a spirit and a method than a mechanical formula or a blind pedagogical technique" (Guerrero). More than academics, the Jesuit system whose one aim has always been "Ad majorem Dei gloriam" made Rizal even more pious and devoted than he was before. And under the watchful eyes of the Jesuits, he bloomed into the Rizal that we know today. "I owe a lot to this Order, almost, almost all that I am taken to be," Rizal later would say. The Ateneo truly, as Craig noted, "carried on the good work which the mother's training had begun".

It was also in Ateneo that Rizal, at least in the memories of his friend Ferdinand Blumentritt, started to feel indignant to racial inequality. Although, as Craig noted, the Jesuit system made no racial distinction and even treated "Filipinos" as Spaniards, Rizal must have still felt discrimination from at least his classmates. He started wondering about the justification for the little regard given to indios like him just because of their color. He even came to the conclusion that indios are intellectually superior to the Spanish students because while the instructions were given in the latters' own language, the indios still managed to compete with them and even sometimes beat them. And beating them was, again according to Blumentritt, one of Rizal's favorites. And since poetry was highly esteemed in Ateneo, he showcased his talents in this field on more than one occasion.

The Bachelor of Arts degree, which was the equivalent to today's high school diploma, that Rizal earned from the Ateneo would not have satisfied a young man whose passion for excellence only a handful could have rivaled. In any case, the family \- his father and elder brother, primarily \- decided to send Rizal to college despite Doña Teodora's protestations.

Rizal wanted to take a course either in literature and the law or in medicine. Paciano dissuaded him from taking up law by pointing out the fact that in the country back then lawyers were landlords, teachers, farmers, and anything else but lawyers, and those who did manage to practice law earned their living from defending one side or the other, regardless of whether they were right or wrong. But he ended up taking a course in philosophy and letters at the University of Santo Tomas because \[Page 78\] Don Francisco Engracio wanted him to study metaphysics. He did, however, simultaneously enroll in the Ateneo for a course in land surveying in response perhaps to his dear Jesuits' encouragement for him to take up agriculture.

His experiences under the Dominicans were a far cry from the ones under the Jesuits. In fact during that first year, he won two medals at the Ateneo while none from the UST. This Rizal himself attributed more to his lack of preparation and enthusiasm, the course being his father's choice and not his, more than to any fault on the part of the Dominicans. But a young mind trained by the Jesuit would always have a tender longing for that kind of training, and this further sharpened whatever contrast there was between the Jesuit and the Dominican systems. For one, the Jesuits' discipline balanced with personal encouragement made him grow while at UST all he observed was the professors' favoritism and ill-treatment for the Filipino students. Of course, to be fair with the Dominicans, Rizal's subpar scholastic performance at the UST could have been caused by other reasons such as, as Guerrero pointed out, medicine being not really his passion \- he enrolled medicine only to cure his mother \- and the distractions that usually accompany men of his age \- it was then that he experienced his first love and lost it even before any affair could start.

Zaide, meanwhile, cited three things that made Rizal unhappy during his stay with the Dominicans: the hostile treatment given to him by the Dominican professors, the racial discrimination suffered by Filipino students which was rampant in the UST back then, and the obsoleteness and repressive nature of the Dominican educational system. An interesting note coming from Craig also cited that the rivalry between UST and Ateneo, and Rizal's being counted as an Atenean even if he was already enrolled in the UST, contributed to the growing sentiments against him. This was highlighted when he won the contest in honor of Cervantes with his poem "El Consejo delos Dioses". He prepared for the contest with the help of the rector of the Ateneo, and his winning it gave pride to the Jesuits and the Ateneo alumni.

There were a number of purported reasons why Rizal left the University of Santo Tomas. The biographers Wenceslao Retana and Gregorio Zaide cited Rizal's inability to endure the stifling atmosphere at the university as the reason for his departure. Guerrero took the more diplomatic approach by citing, aside from the disappointments he experienced in the university, his achievements in literature and the fact that some of his contemporaries were already in Europe as reasons why he left UST. Meanwhile, Craig cited the generic reason of Rizal having to obtain a better \[Page 79\] education that, as Padre Burgos said years earlier, could only be possible in Europe. In any case, he decided to leave UST after finishing the fourth year of his medicine course and, without the knowledge of his family except Paciano, he left for Europe on May 3, 1882\.

Now, perhaps more important than knowing his successes and disappointments, his genius and his averageness, what one can learn from the story of Rizal's journey in education were his eagerness to learn \- for himself and for others, his self-discipline to achieve what he desired, his penchant for giving gratitude where gratitude is due and criticism where criticism is warranted, his taking pride in one's heritage amidst seeming or even real discrimination, and his remembering with fondness the earliest teachings he received from home. \[Page 80\]
